Behind the Scenes of Uganda’s Major Music Festivals: What It Takes to Put on a Festival

Behind the Scenes of Uganda’s Major Music Festivals: What It Takes to Put on a Festival



When a headline performer walks onto a festival stage, the audience sees the finished product: lights, sound, screens, music and thousands of people ready to sing.

What they do not see is the machinery behind it.

Months before the first ticket is scanned, festival organisers are dealing with artists, contracts, venues, permits, security, transport, power, sound, accommodation, vendors, medical teams, waste management and dozens of smaller problems that can become very large problems when thousands of people arrive at once.

That is particularly true for major Ugandan festivals such as Nyege Nyege and Bayimba International Festival of the Arts, which combine music with other forms of entertainment and creative activity.

Nyege Nyege's 2026 programme, for example, advertises seven stages alongside camping, Nile adventures, art installations, workshops, talks, film, fashion and other activities. Bayimba's 2026 festival brings together music, dance, film, theatre, visual arts and creative exchange at Lunkulu Island.

A large festival is therefore less like a long concert and more like a temporary town that has to be designed, staffed, powered, secured and dismantled on schedule.

1. It starts with a festival identity

Before anyone worries about speakers or wristbands, organisers have to decide what the festival actually stands for.

Who is it for?

What kind of music and art belongs there?

What should visitors experience?

Which local artists should be featured? Which international performers fit the concept? How much should the programme focus on established names versus emerging talent?

These decisions shape almost everything that follows.

Nyege Nyege positions itself around contemporary music and art, with a deliberately broad programme extending into fashion, film, performance and other creative disciplines. Bayimba similarly operates as a multidisciplinary arts festival rather than simply a music concert.

This is why festival programming is more complicated than assembling a list of popular musicians.

The organiser is effectively designing a sequence of experiences.

One stage might focus on electronic music while another features traditional or contemporary African sounds. Elsewhere, visitors might encounter a film screening, fashion presentation, workshop, food vendor or art installation.

The lineup is only one piece of the puzzle.

2. Choosing the site changes everything

A conventional concert venue already has some infrastructure. A festival site may have very little.

Organisers might need to create:

  • Stages

  • Audience areas

  • Backstage spaces

  • Artist accommodation

  • Toilets and showers

  • Food and vendor areas

  • Ticketing and entry points

  • Security checkpoints

  • Medical stations

  • Lighting

  • Power distribution

  • Walkways

  • Parking

  • Signage

  • Waste collection points

  • Emergency access routes

The location can make the festival memorable, but it can also make production considerably harder.

Nyege Nyege's 2026 edition is being held at Adrift Overland Camp near Kalagala Falls on the River Nile, with riverside camping and Nile activities. Bayimba's 2026 festival took place at Lunkulu Island on Lake Victoria, where organisers prepared guests for open-air spaces, changing weather and varied terrain.

A spectacular setting therefore comes with practical questions.

Where does equipment arrive?

How do emergency vehicles get through?

What happens if heavy rain turns a walkway into mud?

How do thousands of people leave the site after the final performance?

Where does the rubbish go?

Those questions are considerably less glamorous than booking a headline artist, but they can determine whether an event works.

3. Booking an artist is only the beginning

Seeing an artist's name on a poster can make the booking process look deceptively simple.

Behind that name may be a contract, technical rider, travel arrangements, accommodation, hospitality requirements, payment arrangements, arrival times, security requirements and a schedule for soundcheck.

An international performer may have completely different logistical requirements from a local act travelling from Kampala to Jinja.

The technical rider can be particularly important.

It may specify microphones, instruments, monitors, mixing equipment, stage dimensions, lighting requirements and other technical needs. The production team has to determine what can be supplied locally and what must be brought in.

Timing matters too.

If one artist's equipment takes longer than expected to remove from a stage, the next performance can be delayed. If an artist's vehicle is stuck in traffic, the running order may have to change.

The audience sees a delayed performance.

The production manager sees a chain reaction.

4. Multiple stages create a scheduling puzzle

Large festivals often have several things happening simultaneously.

Nyege Nyege's official 2026 information, for example, lists seven stages.

That means the schedule is not simply:

Artist A → Artist B → Artist C → Headliner.

Instead, several programmes may be running at the same time.

A festival production team has to consider:

  • Artist availability

  • Stage capacity

  • Expected crowd sizes

  • Sound bleed between stages

  • Equipment requirements

  • Soundcheck schedules

  • Changeover times

  • Lighting requirements

  • Transport and arrival times

  • Curfews or operating restrictions

  • Audience movement between stages

A popular act placed on a small stage can create crowd-management problems. Two loud performances positioned too close together can interfere with each other.

Even the running order is therefore a production decision.

5. Sound and lighting have to work across the whole site

A festival's sound system is not simply a collection of very large speakers.

Audio engineers have to make sure performers can hear themselves while audiences receive a suitable mix. They also have to consider what happens when several stages operate simultaneously.

The same applies to lighting.

Lighting designers need to create the visual atmosphere of a performance while also keeping working areas, walkways and other operational spaces sufficiently visible.

Then there are cables, trusses, speakers, lighting rigs and other equipment that have to be positioned without creating unnecessary hazards.

This is one reason festival production involves a surprisingly large technical workforce.

The audience may remember the bass drop.

The crew remembers which cable nearly became a catastrophe.

6. Power is one of the festival's hidden foundations

Everything from stage lighting to ticketing systems depends on electricity.

A festival site can have several competing power requirements:

  • Stages and sound systems

  • Lighting

  • Food vendors

  • Ticketing equipment

  • Mobile-money and payment systems

  • Artist areas

  • Medical facilities

  • Security equipment

  • Communications

  • Charging points

  • Accommodation and camping facilities

Outdoor events therefore need proper power distribution and contingency planning.

A power failure during a quiet afternoon activity is inconvenient.

A power failure during a headline performance, security operation or medical incident is considerably more serious.

For large outdoor events, the production team must also think about where generators or other power infrastructure can safely operate, how equipment is protected from weather and how technicians can reach it quickly.

7. Safety begins long before the gates open

Crowd safety is not something that can be added after the event has been planned.

Uganda Police Force guidance for concerts and entertainment events calls for event clearance, security planning with relevant authorities, suitable emergency exits and assembly points, firefighting equipment, crowd-control measures and medical and emergency arrangements. Police guidance also states that venues should not exceed their capacity.

For organisers, this means thinking about questions such as:

  • How many people are expected?

  • Where do they enter?

  • Where do they exit?

  • Where are emergency vehicles able to enter?

  • How will overcrowding be detected?

  • Where are first-aid facilities?

  • How will lost people or separated groups be handled?

  • What happens if severe weather affects the site?

  • How will security communicate during an incident?

Bayimba's 2026 guest information, for example, states that a medical and first-aid team was stationed on site and outlines a safeguarding system covering harassment, abuse, discrimination and reporting procedures.

That is the less glamorous side of festival production, but arguably one of the most important.

8. Festivals in Uganda also involve permits and local authorities

Organising a major public entertainment event is not simply a matter of renting land and selling tickets.

Uganda has legal and regulatory requirements surrounding public entertainment, while police guidance requires event organisers to obtain clearance and coordinate security arrangements.

The Stage Plays and Public Entertainments Act provides for permits for public entertainment, while Uganda Police Force guidance requires organisers to seek clearance and provide information relevant to crowd and security planning.

The exact requirements can depend on the location, type of event and authorities involved, so organisers need to establish the applicable approvals early rather than treating compliance as a last-minute formality.

For events in Kampala, local authority rules can also affect temporary structures, public spaces, trading, noise and other aspects of event operations. The Kampala Capital City Act gives the Authority powers relating to public entertainment, temporary structures and activities in public places.

This is one area where an aspiring event organiser should obtain current requirements directly from the relevant authorities before advertising an event.

9. Transport becomes part of the festival experience

Getting people to the venue is another logistical challenge.

Imagine several thousand people arriving within a few hours.

The organiser now has to think about:

  • Traffic

  • Parking

  • Drop-off points

  • Shuttle services

  • Public transport connections

  • Pedestrian movement

  • Late arrivals

  • Artist transport

  • Emergency access

  • Safe departure after the event

The challenge becomes even more obvious when the venue is on an island or near a major waterway.

Bayimba's 2026 information included directions through Mukono, Kisoga and Katosi to access points for Lunkulu Island, including boat access. Its guest information also provides transport guidance for guests travelling around the area.

For an island festival, transport is not merely a convenience. It is part of the event infrastructure.

10. Accommodation is another layer of production

A multi-day festival can create an unusual accommodation problem.

Artists may require dedicated accommodation. Production crews may arrive before the public. Some visitors may camp while others use hotels, guesthouses or short-term accommodation in nearby towns.

Nyege Nyege's 2026 festival information advertises camping directly at the festival site. Bayimba's 2026 guest information also includes accommodation arrangements for participating artists.

This distinction matters for ordinary ticket buyers.

A festival ticket does not automatically mean that transport, accommodation, meals, activities or camping are included.

Always check the organiser's current ticket and guest information before travelling.

11. Vendors turn a festival into a temporary marketplace

Walk through a large festival and the music is only part of what is happening commercially.

There may be:

  • Food vendors

  • Drink sellers

  • Fashion businesses

  • Craft sellers

  • Photographers

  • Beauty businesses

  • Mobile-money services

  • Merchandise sellers

  • Transport operators

  • Accommodation providers

  • Temporary workers

  • Security personnel

  • Cleaners

  • Technicians

The opportunity can be significant for small businesses, but festival vending is not automatically profitable.

A vendor may have to pay for space, stock, transport, staff, packaging and equipment before selling a single item.

Bayimba's 2026 vendor information provides a useful real-world example: published participation fees vary by category, with listed spaces for areas such as food, fashion, crafts, beauty, merchandise, telecommunications and interactive experiences. Some packages include items such as tables, chairs, electricity, water or security.

For a Ugandan small business considering festival vending, the sensible calculation is not simply:

Expected sales − stall fee = profit

It is closer to:

Sales − stock − stall fee − transport − staff − packaging − payment costs − other expenses = actual profit

That distinction can save a vendor from an expensive weekend.

12. Mobile money and connectivity matter

Uganda's cashless payment ecosystem creates another consideration for festivals.

Vendors and organisers may use mobile-money services and electronic payments, but large crowds can place pressure on mobile networks and devices.

Bayimba's 2026 guest information specifically advises international participants that mobile data is more reliable than Wi-Fi on Lunkulu Island and notes that UGX cash is necessary for many island transactions.

For visitors, carrying an appropriate backup payment option can therefore be sensible.

For vendors, it is worth planning for both digital and cash transactions where appropriate and legal.

13. Medical teams are part of the production crew

Medical provision is easy to overlook when thinking about music festivals.

Large crowds, heat, alcohol, exhaustion, falls, minor injuries, dehydration and other incidents can all create medical demand.

Water-based venues introduce additional considerations.

A responsible festival needs clear procedures for finding medical help, communicating incidents and getting emergency assistance when necessary.

Bayimba's 2026 artist information states that a medical and first-aid team was stationed continuously on site and advises participants with medical conditions or allergies to carry necessary medication and inform the festival team.

Visitors should take similar precautions.

If you take prescription medication, bring enough for the trip. If you are attending an outdoor festival for several days, hydration, rest and appropriate clothing are not particularly exciting advice, but they are rather more useful than discovering your limits at 2 a.m.

14. Water-side festivals require extra caution

Uganda's major festivals sometimes make use of spectacular natural settings.

That can be part of the attraction, but it also changes the risk profile.

At a festival near the Nile or Lake Victoria, visitors should follow designated access routes and obey instructions around water activities.

Do not assume that because other people are entering the water, every part of the shoreline is safe.

Nyege Nyege's 2026 programme advertises activities including white-water rafting, bungee jumping and sunset cruises, while Bayimba's festival takes place on Lunkulu Island. These activities require their own operators, safety procedures and visitor responsibilities.

The festival atmosphere should not switch off common sense.

15. Waste management starts before the first song

Thousands of people produce a considerable amount of waste.

Food packaging, bottles, cups, paper, disposable containers and other materials have to be collected and removed.

The organiser therefore needs to plan:

  • Bin locations

  • Waste collection

  • Toilets

  • Cleaning crews

  • Waste storage

  • Water access

  • Site restoration

  • Vendor responsibilities

The environmental challenge is particularly important for festivals held on islands, riverbanks and other natural sites.

Bayimba describes Lunkulu Island as an eco-friendly setting and explicitly incorporates sustainability into its festival identity.

The principle is simple: a festival should leave behind an event site, not a rubbish dump.

16. What happens on a typical festival day?

A festival day can look chaotic from the audience's perspective, but the production team usually works from a detailed schedule.

Stage of the dayWhat is happening behind the scenes
Early morningCleaning, security checks, technical inspections and site preparation
Before openingArtist arrivals, soundchecks, equipment checks and staff briefings
Gates openTicket checks, security screening, crowd movement and vendor operations
During performancesStage changeovers, artist support, crowd monitoring, medical response and technical troubleshooting
Between major setsEquipment movement, lighting changes, sound adjustments and schedule coordination
Late nightSecurity, transport coordination, artist movement and crowd dispersal
After closingCleaning, equipment protection, incident reporting and site checks
Next morningRepairs, restocking, waste collection and preparation for the next programme

The exact schedule varies from one festival to another.

The important point is that the festival does not stop when the music stops.

17. What can go wrong?

Festival production is essentially professional problem-solving under time pressure.

Common operational problems can include:

  • Artist delays

  • Equipment failure

  • Power problems

  • Bad weather

  • Traffic congestion

  • Overcrowding

  • Connectivity problems

  • Vendor shortages

  • Medical incidents

  • Lost property

  • Transport delays

  • Waste-management problems

  • Changes to the performance schedule

The public may experience one of these as a five-minute delay.

Behind the scenes, a production manager may be coordinating several teams simultaneously to prevent that five-minute delay from becoming an hour.

That is why contingency planning matters.

A good festival does not assume everything will go perfectly. It prepares for the things that probably will not.

18. How much does it cost to put on a festival?

There is no reliable single figure for the cost of producing a major Ugandan music festival.

Budgets vary enormously according to venue, duration, audience size, number of stages, artist fees, production quality, security requirements, transport arrangements and whether infrastructure already exists.

A festival budget may include:

Cost areaExamples
ArtistsPerformance fees, travel, accommodation and hospitality
ProductionStages, sound, lighting, screens and technical crews
VenueSite hire and related charges
InfrastructureToilets, tents, fencing, temporary structures and utilities
PowerGenerators, fuel, distribution and technicians
SecuritySecurity personnel, access control and related equipment
MedicalFirst aid, medical personnel and emergency arrangements
TransportArtist movement, staff transport, shuttles and logistics
MarketingDigital advertising, media, design and promotional materials
StaffProduction, administration, cleaning, hospitality and support
WasteBins, collection, cleaning and disposal
Insurance and complianceApplicable permits, insurance and other regulatory costs

Because festival organisers do not generally publish complete production budgets, any precise “a major Ugandan festival costs X billion shillings” claim should be treated cautiously unless the organiser itself has disclosed the figure.

19. Can ordinary Ugandans work at music festivals?

Yes. Festivals create temporary and specialist work across several areas.

Potential roles include:

  • Event assistants

  • Security personnel

  • Cleaners

  • Drivers

  • Stagehands

  • Sound technicians

  • Lighting technicians

  • Photographers

  • Videographers

  • Ushers

  • Vendor staff

  • Hospitality workers

  • Medical personnel

  • Production assistants

  • Social-media teams

  • Brand activation staff

Some positions require specialist qualifications or experience. Others are temporary operational roles where reliability, communication and physical stamina matter more than formal academic credentials.

If you want to enter Uganda's events industry, volunteering or taking temporary festival work can provide useful exposure to how live events actually operate.

It is also worth developing a specific skill rather than trying to become a vague “event person”. Sound engineering, lighting, stage management, photography, videography, logistics, digital marketing and event technology can each become careers in their own right.

20. What festival-goers should do before attending

You can make your own festival experience considerably easier by doing a little preparation.

Check the official information

Use the organiser's official website or stated ticketing platform for current dates, prices, schedules, transport arrangements and venue information.

Do not rely entirely on an old poster circulating on WhatsApp.

Plan your transport

Know how you are getting there and, more importantly, how you are getting back.

If the venue is outside Kampala, allow for traffic and limited late-night transport.

Carry essentials

Depending on the event, useful items may include:

  • Phone and power bank

  • Identification

  • Necessary medication

  • Rain protection

  • Comfortable shoes

  • Insect repellent

  • Drinking water where permitted

  • Cash or an approved backup payment method

Know where help is available

When you arrive, take a moment to identify exits, security personnel and medical facilities.

It is much easier to find the medical point before you need it.

Respect the site

Do not block emergency routes, damage vegetation, litter or enter restricted areas.

And if the festival is near water, follow the site's instructions rather than treating the shoreline as an extension of the dance floor.

The bigger picture: a festival is a temporary ecosystem

The easiest way to understand a major Ugandan music festival is to stop thinking about it as a stage.

The stage is merely the most visible part.

Behind it are programmers choosing artists, production managers building schedules, technicians controlling sound and lighting, logistics teams moving people and equipment, security teams managing crowds, medical workers handling emergencies, vendors serving thousands of customers, cleaners maintaining the site and temporary workers keeping everything moving.

Then there are the audiences themselves.

Every successful festival is therefore a network of people and systems working at roughly the same time.

Nyege Nyege's seven-stage 2026 programme and Bayimba's multidisciplinary island festival illustrate how far these events have moved beyond the traditional idea of a one-night concert.

The next time the lights go down and the headliner walks onto the stage, remember that the performance is only the visible tip of the operation.

The real festival has been running for months.
