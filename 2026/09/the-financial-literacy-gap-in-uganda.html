The Financial Literacy Gap in Uganda: Who Is Responsible?



When someone loses money to a scam, takes an expensive loan or reaches the end of the month with nothing left, the usual reaction is simple: they should have known better.

Sometimes that is fair. People have a responsibility to understand the financial decisions they make, protect their accounts and avoid obviously reckless offers.

But that explanation is too convenient for Uganda's financial-literacy problem.

Financial decisions are not made in a vacuum. A person's income, education, family responsibilities, access to financial services, digital skills and the quality of information provided by financial institutions all affect what they can realistically do with their money.

That raises a more useful question than "Who is to blame?"

Who is responsible for making financial decisions safer and easier to understand?

The answer includes individuals, banks, mobile-money providers, lenders, insurers, schools, employers, regulators, government and even the media.

Uganda is becoming more financially included, but that is not the same as being financially capable

Uganda has made considerable progress in financial inclusion.

The 2023 FinScope Uganda survey found that 81% of adults were financially included, compared with 77% in 2018. Formal financial inclusion increased from 58% to 68% over the same period.

Mobile money has been a major part of this expansion. Village Savings and Loan Associations (VSLAs) and other informal financial arrangements also remain important.

But there is an important distinction:

Having access to a financial service does not necessarily mean knowing how to use it well.

Someone can have a mobile-money account without understanding how to recognise a social-engineering scam.

Someone can qualify for a digital loan without understanding the total cost of borrowing.

Someone can have a bank account without comparing fees or knowing how to make a complaint.

Someone can join an investment scheme without understanding the difference between a legitimate investment and an unrealistic promise of guaranteed returns.

Financial inclusion answers the question: Can people access and use financial services?

Financial literacy asks a different question: Do people have the knowledge and confidence to make informed decisions about those services?

Uganda needs both.

The numbers also show that financial opportunity is not evenly distributed

FinScope 2023 found substantial differences in formal financial inclusion.

GroupFormal financial inclusion
All adults68%
Men75%
Women62%
Urban adults81%
Rural adults62%

The survey covered Ugandans aged 16 and above and was nationally representative.

These differences matter because financial literacy cannot be separated completely from financial circumstances.

A salaried worker in Kampala with a bank account, smartphone, stable income and access to several financial institutions faces a different financial environment from a rural trader whose income changes from week to week.

Likewise, someone with limited formal education may need financial information presented very differently from someone with university training.

That does not mean people with lower incomes or less education cannot make good financial decisions. It means financial education has to account for the conditions in which people actually make those decisions.

What should individuals be responsible for?

Shared responsibility does not mean individuals have no responsibility.

Consumers still need to develop basic financial habits.

Before taking a loan, for example, a borrower should ask:

  • How much will I receive?

  • How much will I repay in total?

  • What is the interest rate and how is it calculated?

  • Are there additional fees?

  • What happens if I pay late?

  • What happens if I miss several payments?

  • Is the lender properly authorised?

  • Can I afford the repayment without sacrificing essential expenses?

The same principle applies to investments.

Before sending money to an investment platform, individual or group, ask:

  • Who operates it?

  • Is the provider regulated where regulation applies?

  • Where does the promised return supposedly come from?

  • What are the risks?

  • Can I withdraw my money, and under what conditions?

  • What happens if the investment loses value?

  • Is anyone promising an unusually high or guaranteed return?

People should also protect their mobile-money PINs, banking credentials, passwords and one-time passwords.

And basic money management still matters: tracking spending, separating essential expenses from discretionary spending, saving for predictable costs and avoiding unnecessary debt can make a substantial difference.

But there is an important limit.

"Consumers should be careful" cannot become an excuse for poor disclosure, misleading advertising or weak complaint systems.

Financial literacy is difficult when income itself is unstable

One of the biggest problems with generic financial advice is that it often assumes a predictable monthly salary.

That is not how everyone in Uganda earns money.

FinScope 2023 found trading to be the leading reported source of income among adults, alongside casual labour, services and wages or salaries.

For a market trader, boda boda operator, farmer, casual worker or small business owner, the problem may not simply be "you don't know how to budget."

Cash flow itself can be unpredictable.

A household may receive substantial income after a good harvest or strong trading period and then experience weeks of weak income. School fees, medical expenses, rent, food and family obligations do not necessarily wait for the next profitable month.

Financial education for irregular-income households therefore needs to cover issues such as:

  • separating household and business money;

  • keeping enough working capital for the business;

  • planning for school fees and other predictable expenses;

  • building an emergency reserve during better months;

  • avoiding expensive emergency borrowing;

  • understanding the risks of guaranteeing another person's loan;

  • comparing savings and credit options;

  • keeping basic business records.

That is more useful than simply telling someone to "save 20% of your salary" when they do not actually receive a fixed salary.

What responsibility do banks and financial-service providers have?

Financial institutions have considerably more information about their products than most customers do.

That creates an information imbalance.

A bank, lender, insurer, mobile-money provider or investment business knows its fees, conditions, penalties and operational procedures. A customer may not.

That is why financial consumer protection matters.

Bank of Uganda's consumer-protection framework emphasises transparency, fair treatment, disclosure and mechanisms for handling complaints. Its current financial-inclusion work also identifies consumer protection and financial literacy as important parts of a functioning financial system.

Good disclosure should not mean handing customers a huge document and assuming the job is finished.

A customer should be able to understand the practical consequences of a product.

For a loan, that means understanding the actual cost of borrowing.

For insurance, it means understanding important exclusions and claim conditions.

For savings, it means knowing applicable charges, access conditions and any restrictions.

For digital financial services, it means knowing how transactions are secured and what to do when something goes wrong.

A customer should not need a law degree to discover what a financial product will cost them.

Digital finance creates new opportunities and new risks

Uganda's expansion of mobile money and digital financial services has made financial services much more accessible.

But convenience can also make poor decisions faster.

A person can transfer money in seconds. They can also send money to a fraudster in seconds.

Digital lending can provide emergency funds quickly, but the speed of approval can make it tempting to borrow before thinking through repayment.

Fraudsters also exploit urgency.

Messages claiming that an account will be blocked, a prize has been won or an urgent payment is required are designed to make people act before thinking.

Financial literacy therefore increasingly includes digital financial literacy.

People need to understand not only interest and savings, but also phishing, impersonation, account security, fake investment platforms, fraudulent links and the importance of verifying who is requesting money.

The National Financial Inclusion Strategy II also recognises digital financial services, consumer protection and financial literacy as connected parts of financial inclusion.

What should schools teach about money?

Financial literacy should not begin when someone receives their first salary.

Schools can introduce practical concepts such as:

  • budgeting;

  • saving;

  • interest;

  • borrowing;

  • risk;

  • insurance;

  • digital payments;

  • fraud awareness;

  • distinguishing needs from wants;

  • evaluating financial claims.

The emphasis should be on practical decisions rather than memorising definitions.

For example, instead of simply defining compound interest, students could compare two hypothetical savings or loan scenarios.

Instead of merely defining fraud, they could examine a fake mobile-money message and identify the warning signs.

Instead of discussing budgeting abstractly, they could build a monthly budget using a realistic Ugandan income and household expenses.

That kind of education is much closer to the decisions people will eventually face.

Employers have a role too

The workplace is another practical point at which financial education can reach adults.

An employee receiving a regular salary may suddenly have to manage rent, transport, food, family support, loans, savings and retirement contributions.

Employers can help workers understand:

  • payslips and deductions;

  • workplace benefits;

  • retirement contributions;

  • savings options;

  • responsible borrowing;

  • basic fraud and digital-security practices.

This does not mean an employer should tell workers where to invest their money.

The useful role is helping employees understand the financial decisions they are already facing.

Why women and rural households need particular attention

Financial-literacy programmes cannot assume that every Ugandan has the same access to money and financial institutions.

FinScope 2023 found formal financial inclusion at 62% among women compared with 75% among men. Rural formal inclusion was 62%, compared with 81% in urban areas.

The survey also found that informal financial inclusion was particularly important among women and rural populations.

These differences can reflect a combination of factors, including income, location, access to formal services, education and the type of work people do.

This is why financial education delivered only through formal workplaces, urban institutions or online platforms will not necessarily reach the people who need it most.

Community groups, VSLAs, SACCOs, schools, local organisations and other trusted channels can be important routes for reaching people outside the traditional banking environment.

What should government and regulators be doing?

Government and regulators cannot make every financial decision for citizens, but they can shape the environment in which those decisions are made.

Uganda's National Financial Inclusion Strategy II for 2023–2028 identifies five major areas:

  1. Reducing barriers to formal financial services.

  2. Increasing the use of affordable and quality formal financial products.

  3. Strengthening financial consumer protection and financial capability.

  4. Developing an inclusive green-finance market.

  5. Promoting gender-inclusive finance.

The strategy aims for broader access to quality, affordable and responsibly delivered formal financial services.

The Ministry of Finance reported in 2025 that the strategy had a target of reaching at least 85% financial inclusion by 2028.

As of September 2026, the strategy is also undergoing a mid-term review, with the National Curriculum Development Centre engaging with Bank of Uganda on the role of education and financial literacy.

The regulatory job is therefore broader than simply publishing financial-education material.

It includes supervising financial institutions, supporting consumer protection, improving transparency, making complaint mechanisms workable and helping consumers understand their rights.

Financial literacy cannot solve poverty by itself

This is one of the most important points in the debate.

Suppose two people receive UGX 500,000 a month.

One spends almost all of it on food, rent, transport and family obligations. The other has the same income but fewer dependants and lower living costs.

Giving both people the same budgeting lesson does not create the same financial outcome.

Likewise, telling someone with highly irregular income to "build a six-month emergency fund" may be technically sound advice but practically difficult.

Financial literacy can improve decision-making. It cannot manufacture income.

Low wages, unemployment, high living costs, unexpected medical bills, school fees and family obligations can all limit how much room a household has to save.

This is why financial literacy should be treated as one part of financial resilience, not a substitute for adequate income or economic opportunity.

So, who is responsible?

The better answer is shared responsibility.

ActorWhat they are responsible for
IndividualsAsking questions, protecting accounts, comparing products, keeping records and making decisions they understand
Banks and lendersClear pricing, responsible communication, fair treatment and accessible complaints processes
Mobile-money and digital providersClear information, security, fraud awareness and effective support when transactions go wrong
Insurers and investment providersExplaining risks, conditions, charges and limitations clearly
SchoolsTeaching practical money skills before students enter adult financial life
EmployersHelping workers understand salaries, deductions, benefits and basic financial decisions
Government and regulatorsCreating and enforcing rules, supervising providers and strengthening consumer protection
Community organisationsDelivering practical financial education through trusted local networks
Media and influencersChecking financial claims before promoting products or opportunities

None of these responsibilities eliminates the others.

A consumer who ignores obvious warnings still has responsibility.

A provider that hides important costs cannot reasonably shift all responsibility onto the customer.

A regulator cannot guarantee that nobody will ever be scammed.

And financial education cannot fix an income that is simply too small to cover basic needs.

What would a better financial-literacy programme in Uganda look like?

A useful programme would be less concerned with teaching financial vocabulary and more concerned with improving real decisions.

It could teach people how to:

Compare a loan.
Put two borrowing offers side by side and calculate what each will actually cost.

Check a financial provider.
Know where to look for information about regulated institutions and when to be suspicious of an unverified provider.

Spot a scam.
Recognise pressure, secrecy, guaranteed returns, fake authority and requests for PINs or passwords.

Build a realistic budget.
Use actual income and expenses rather than an imaginary middle-class salary.

Plan irregular income.
Separate business cash, household spending, savings and money reserved for predictable expenses.

Use mobile money safely.
Verify recipients, protect PINs and know what to do when a transaction goes wrong.

Understand insurance.
Know that paying a premium does not mean every possible loss is automatically covered.

Prepare for old age.
Understand the purpose of retirement savings and the consequences of ignoring long-term financial planning.

These are skills people can use immediately.

The real financial-literacy gap is bigger than knowledge

Uganda does have a financial-literacy problem, but calling it simply a "knowledge gap" can be misleading.

There is also an information gap, where customers may not have clear information.

There is an access gap, where useful services are unavailable, inconvenient or unaffordable.

There is an income gap, where households have too little financial room to implement otherwise sensible advice.

There is a digital-skills gap, where people can use mobile money but may struggle to identify sophisticated fraud.

And there is a trust gap, where consumers may not know which financial information or providers they can safely rely on.

FinScope 2023 shows that Uganda has made substantial progress in financial inclusion. The next challenge is making that inclusion more useful, safe and understandable.

The bottom line

Uganda's financial-literacy gap cannot fairly be pinned entirely on individuals.

People should learn how money works, ask questions, protect their accounts and think carefully before borrowing or investing.

But financial institutions also have a duty to communicate clearly. Schools and employers can teach practical skills. Community organisations can reach people outside formal institutions. Regulators and government have to enforce standards and make consumer protection meaningful.

Most importantly, financial literacy should not be confused with financial prosperity.

A financially knowledgeable person can still struggle when income is low, employment is uncertain or family obligations consume most of their earnings.

The goal, therefore, should not simply be to teach Ugandans how to "make better choices."

It should be to create a financial environment in which people have the information, options, protection and economic room needed to make those choices well.

That is a much harder task than blaming someone after the money is gone. It is also far more useful.
