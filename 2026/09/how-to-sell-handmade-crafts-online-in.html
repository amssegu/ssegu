How to Sell Handmade Crafts Online in Uganda: E-Commerce Guide

Learn how to sell handmade crafts online in Uganda using WhatsApp, Instagram, TikTok and e-commerce. Covers pricing, payments, delivery, taxes, steps

SSEGUPost2026-09-20

 

From Craft Markets to E-Commerce: How Uganda’s Handmade Businesses Can Sell Online



For years, many Ugandan craft sellers have depended on physical markets, tourist shops, roadside stalls, exhibitions and word-of-mouth referrals. A customer sees a basket, piece of beadwork, fabric item or carved decoration, asks the price and either buys it or walks away.

That model has not disappeared. But the way customers discover products is changing.

A craft maker in Kampala can now photograph a basket, post it on WhatsApp or Instagram, answer questions in a chat and arrange payment and delivery without the buyer ever visiting the workshop. A seller in Jinja, Masaka or Fort Portal can similarly reach customers beyond the local market.

Uganda's digital market is large enough to make this worthwhile. By December 2025, the Uganda Communications Commission reported 47.1 million active mobile subscriptions, 36.3 million active mobile-money accounts and 18.5 million internet subscriptions. WhatsApp alone had about 11.4 million subscriptions, while TikTok had 11.3 million and Instagram 1.5 million. These figures do not mean every account represents a unique person or a potential buyer, but they show the scale of the digital channels available to small businesses.

For craft businesses, however, moving online is not simply a matter of posting photographs.

The seller still has to make the product, calculate its real cost, answer customers, receive payment, package the order, arrange delivery and deal with returns or complaints.

The opportunity is real. So is the operational work behind it.

Why e-commerce can work for Uganda’s craft businesses

Handmade products have one major advantage online: they are highly visual.

A good photograph can communicate the colour, texture, shape and size of a basket or piece of beadwork before the customer sees it physically. A short video can show how an item is made or demonstrate its size.

Online selling can also remove some of the geographical limitations of a physical stall.

Instead of relying entirely on people who happen to pass your shop or exhibition stand, a craft maker can build an audience of customers interested in:

  • African home décor

  • handmade baskets

  • Ugandan souvenirs

  • jewellery and beadwork

  • woven products

  • traditional fabrics

  • pottery and ceramics

  • carved wooden products

  • handmade gifts

  • corporate and event gifts

The customer may still want to collect the item physically, but the discovery and ordering process can happen online.

That hybrid model is particularly relevant in Uganda. The U.S. International Trade Administration describes Uganda's e-commerce market as still relatively informal, with buyers and sellers commonly connecting online and completing transactions through phone calls, physical meetings, cash or mobile money.

In other words, you do not need to build the Ugandan version of Amazon before you can start selling online.

You do not need a complicated website to start

One mistake small businesses make is assuming that e-commerce means immediately paying for a sophisticated online shop.

It doesn't.

A craft business can start with:

  1. A well-organised WhatsApp Business catalogue

  2. An Instagram or TikTok account

  3. Clear product photographs

  4. A simple order process

  5. Mobile-money or other suitable payment options

  6. A reliable delivery arrangement

  7. A spreadsheet or notebook recording orders and stock

A website becomes more useful as the catalogue, customer base and order volume grow.

The important distinction is between having an online presence and having an organised online sales process.

A beautiful Instagram page with no prices, vague descriptions and slow replies is not a particularly good shop.

Uganda is already moving towards digital craft commerce

There is also evidence of institutional interest in taking Uganda's crafts and souvenirs sector online.

In March 2025, the National Information Technology Authority-Uganda (NITA-U) published a procurement notice for the development and deployment of an e-commerce platform for Uganda's crafts and souvenirs industry. The project was funded through the World Bank-supported Uganda Digital Acceleration Project.

That does not mean every Ugandan artisan is already selling through a national platform. It also does not eliminate the need for individual businesses to build their own customer relationships.

What it does show is that digital commerce is being considered as part of the development of Uganda's crafts and souvenirs industry.

For an individual maker, the sensible approach is still to start small, learn what customers actually buy and improve the sales process gradually.

Build product listings that answer the customer's questions

One of the biggest weaknesses in online craft selling is incomplete product information.

A photograph might look excellent, but the buyer still needs to know:

  • What exactly is the item?

  • What is it made from?

  • How large is it?

  • How much does it weigh, approximately?

  • Is every piece identical?

  • Is it handmade or partly machine-made?

  • What colours are available?

  • Is it currently in stock?

  • Can it be customised?

  • How long does a made-to-order item take?

  • How much does it cost?

  • Does the price include delivery?

  • Where can it be delivered?

  • How can the customer pay?

A good listing should answer most of these questions before the customer has to ask.

Example of a stronger listing

Handwoven Ugandan Basket – Medium

  • Material: natural fibre

  • Approximate size: 35 cm diameter × 25 cm high

  • Use: storage, décor or gifting

  • Colour: natural fibre with coloured detailing

  • Price: UGX 65,000

  • Availability: 4 in stock

  • Delivery: Kampala and other locations available at an additional fee

  • Custom orders: available on request

  • Handmade: minor variations in colour, shape and pattern may occur

The details can vary by product. The principle is simple: do not make the customer interrogate the seller to understand what they are buying.

Take photographs that sell the product honestly

You do not necessarily need an expensive camera.

A modern smartphone, natural light and a clean background can produce useful product photographs.

For each important product, consider taking:

  • one full front photograph;

  • one side or alternative-angle photograph;

  • one close-up showing texture or workmanship;

  • one photograph showing the product next to a familiar object for scale;

  • one photograph showing how the product is used;

  • a short video where movement or size matters.

Avoid making the product look substantially different from reality.

If a basket is 30 cm wide, do not photograph it in a way that makes it appear large enough to fill a room.

Likewise, if handmade materials naturally produce differences between individual pieces, explain that upfront.

That reduces one of the most common problems in online selling: the customer received what the seller sent, but expected something different.

Price the craft, not just the materials

A handmade business can become surprisingly unprofitable when the maker calculates only the cost of materials.

Suppose a basket requires:

  • UGX 18,000 in materials

  • UGX 15,000 worth of labour

  • UGX 3,000 packaging

  • UGX 4,000 payment, marketing or other selling costs

The production and selling cost is already approximately UGX 40,000 before profit.

If the maker sells it for UGX 42,000 because "the materials only cost UGX 18,000", the business is effectively paying the maker very little for their time.

A useful starting formula is:

Materials + labour + packaging + selling costs + delivery handling + overheads + profit = selling price

Not every business needs to include delivery in the advertised price. In fact, it may be clearer to state:

UGX 65,000 + delivery

rather than quietly increasing the product price because the customer happens to live far away.

For businesses selling across Uganda, delivery charges can vary significantly by destination and courier, so quote them clearly rather than promising "delivery available everywhere" without knowing the actual cost.

Keep your catalogue separate from social media

Social media should be treated as a shop window, not the only copy of your business records.

Keep a master catalogue containing:

Beaded necklaceBDL-002UGX 12,000UGX 25,0007SavedAvailable
Large wall basketBSK-003UGX 55,000UGX 90,0000SavedMade to order

This can initially be a Google Sheet or Excel file.

The important thing is that there is one place where the current information lives.

This prevents problems such as:

  • an old Instagram post showing the wrong price;

  • selling an item that is already out of stock;

  • forgetting what a customer ordered;

  • losing product photographs;

  • confusing similar colour variations;

  • not knowing which products actually make money.

Back up the catalogue and photographs rather than keeping everything on one phone.

Use WhatsApp as part of the sales system

For many small Ugandan businesses, WhatsApp is particularly useful because the customer can discover the product, ask questions and place an order in the same conversation.

A simple order format can make the process much cleaner:

Order number: UG-104
Product: Medium basket BSK-001
Quantity: 2
Product total: UGX 130,000
Delivery: UGX 8,000
Total: UGX 138,000
Payment status: Paid
Delivery: Kampala
Expected delivery: 19 September

The seller does not need sophisticated software to begin doing this.

The important part is consistency.

Make payment confirmation part of the process

Mobile money makes online commerce easier in Uganda, but payment handling still requires discipline.

Do not dispatch an order merely because a customer sends a screenshot claiming that payment has been made.

Confirm that the money has actually reached the intended account or wallet before releasing the product.

For every paid order, record:

  • order number;

  • amount expected;

  • amount received;

  • payment date;

  • payment method;

  • customer;

  • product;

  • delivery destination;

  • dispatch status.

For larger businesses, separate business finances from personal spending as much as possible.

It becomes very difficult to determine whether a craft business is profitable when sales revenue, rent, food, airtime and household spending all disappear into the same wallet.

Delivery can make or break the customer experience

A beautiful product can still produce a bad review if it arrives late, damaged or at an unexpected cost.

Before advertising nationwide delivery, test your delivery process.

Start with destinations you can reliably serve, then expand.

For each order, confirm:

  1. Customer's name

  2. Phone number

  3. Exact delivery location

  4. Landmark where useful

  5. Product and quantity

  6. Total amount

  7. Delivery fee

  8. Expected delivery date

  9. Payment status

  10. Courier or delivery person

For fragile products, use appropriate packaging and photograph the packaged parcel before handover.

If a third-party courier is involved, understand what happens if the package is lost, damaged or the customer cannot be reached.

Do not leave these questions until there is already a dispute.

Social media is your marketing channel, not your entire business

Uganda's digital audience gives craft sellers several places to showcase products.

UCC's December 2025 figures put WhatsApp and TikTok at roughly 11 million subscriptions each, followed by YouTube and other platforms.

That does not mean every craft business needs to maintain five social-media accounts.

Choose the channels where your target customers actually spend time.

A useful content mix might include:

Product content

Show the finished item, price, dimensions and how to order.

Making-process content

Show weaving, carving, beadwork, pottery or finishing.

This gives customers a better understanding of why handmade products may cost more than mass-produced alternatives.

Educational content

Explain the material, cultural significance, care instructions or appropriate use.

Customer content

With permission, show finished orders in their new homes or workplaces.

Behind-the-scenes content

Show the workshop, tools and people involved in producing the items.

The objective is not to post endlessly.

It is to give potential customers enough information and confidence to make a purchase.

Protect your designs and photographs

Online exposure creates another problem: copying.

A seller may discover that photographs of their baskets, jewellery or designs have been downloaded and reused by another business.

Uganda's intellectual-property framework provides protection for creations including artistic works, designs, names and images used in commerce. The Uganda Registration Services Bureau provides IP registration services, including copyright and trademarks.

URSB also announced in 2025 that the requirement to gazette copyright applications was removed under new regulations, making the registration process less cumbersome.

For a serious craft business, keep dated originals of:

  • product photographs;

  • design sketches;

  • patterns;

  • branding materials;

  • packaging designs;

  • agreements with designers or other makers.

Do not use another business's photographs simply because they are easy to find online.

If you commission a photographer, designer or other creator, clarify who owns the resulting work and what each party is allowed to use.

Be careful with customer information

An online craft business may collect names, phone numbers, delivery locations and payment-related information.

That information should not be treated casually.

Uganda's Data Protection and Privacy Act establishes obligations for organisations handling personal data, and the Personal Data Protection Office provides guidance for data collectors, processors and controllers.

In practical terms, a small seller should:

  • collect only information needed for the transaction;

  • keep customer information private;

  • avoid posting customer phone numbers publicly;

  • do not share delivery details unnecessarily;

  • ask permission before using a customer's photograph or testimonial;

  • delete information that no longer needs to be retained where appropriate.

A customer's delivery address is not marketing content.

Do Ugandan craft sellers need to think about taxes?

Yes.

Selling handmade products online does not automatically make a business tax-free simply because the seller operates through WhatsApp, Instagram or a website.

URA currently defines a small business for its presumptive-tax regime as one with annual sales above UGX 10 million and not exceeding UGX 150 million, subject to the applicable rules and exclusions. Businesses with annual sales not exceeding UGX 10 million have a nil rate under the published small-business schedule.

VAT registration is a separate issue. URA states that businesses making taxable supplies above UGX 150 million in annual sales fall into the VAT-registration category.

Tax treatment can depend on the structure and circumstances of the business, so sellers approaching these levels should check their obligations with URA rather than relying on advice from social media.

It is also worth keeping proper sales records from the beginning. Tax compliance becomes considerably less painful when the business already knows how much it sold.

Some craft products also have standards

Not every handmade product has the same regulatory requirements.

The Uganda National Bureau of Standards has standards covering particular categories of craft products. For example, UNBS lists voluntary standards for handwoven baskets, handwoven mats and ceramic or pottery handicrafts.

This matters especially when moving beyond individual consumer sales into hotels, retailers, corporate buyers or export markets.

A buyer supplying a hotel or retailer may care about consistent dimensions, materials, packaging, quality and quantities in ways that an individual tourist buying one basket may not.

Check the relevant UNBS requirements for the specific product rather than assuming every craft item is governed by the same rules.

Start with 10 to 15 products, not 100

A new online seller does not need to digitise the entire workshop immediately.

Start with the products that are:

  • consistently available;

  • relatively easy to photograph;

  • profitable to produce;

  • straightforward to package;

  • unlikely to break during delivery;

  • already receiving customer interest.

Create detailed listings for perhaps 10 to 15 products.

Then monitor what happens.

Which products receive enquiries?

Which ones actually sell?

Which ones generate repeat purchases?

Which products produce too many delivery problems?

Which products take three hours to make but produce almost no profit?

The answers are more valuable than follower counts.

A practical 30-day plan for a Ugandan craft business

Week 1: Organise the products

Choose your first 10 to 15 products.

Give each product a code.

Record:

  • material cost;

  • labour time;

  • packaging cost;

  • selling price;

  • available stock;

  • made-to-order time.

Week 2: Create the catalogue

Photograph each product.

Write complete descriptions.

Add dimensions, prices, variations and delivery information.

Save the master catalogue somewhere backed up.

Week 3: Start selling

Choose one primary channel, such as WhatsApp Business, Instagram or TikTok.

Publish consistently.

Respond to enquiries promptly.

Use an order number for every confirmed sale.

Week 4: Measure what actually happened

Review:

  • number of enquiries;

  • number of completed orders;

  • most-requested products;

  • average order value;

  • delivery problems;

  • cancellations;

  • customer questions;

  • actual profit.

Then improve the catalogue.

This is much more useful than judging the business solely by likes and followers.

When should you build a website?

A website becomes more attractive when customers repeatedly ask:

  • "Where can I see all your products?"

  • "Do you have a price list?"

  • "Can I order online?"

  • "Can I pay by card?"

  • "Do you deliver outside Kampala?"

  • "Do you export?"

  • "Can I buy several items for my company?"

At that stage, a website can become the business's central catalogue rather than simply another marketing expense.

It can contain:

  • product categories;

  • prices;

  • product specifications;

  • stock information;

  • delivery policies;

  • payment instructions;

  • frequently asked questions;

  • contact information;

  • business story;

  • wholesale enquiries.

But do not build the website first and figure out the business afterwards.

A website cannot fix poor pricing, unreliable stock records or a chaotic delivery process.

What about selling to customers outside Uganda?

International sales can create opportunities for Ugandan handmade products, particularly where the product has a distinctive story or design.

But exporting introduces additional considerations:

  • international shipping costs;

  • packaging requirements;

  • customs documentation;

  • destination-country import rules;

  • payment and foreign-exchange arrangements;

  • returns;

  • product restrictions;

  • consistent production quality.

Start by learning to fulfil local orders reliably.

Then test regional or international orders with products that are relatively light, durable and easy to package.

A basket that costs UGX 60,000 to make may be attractive to an overseas customer, but that does not automatically make it profitable to export. Shipping can change the economics completely.

Common mistakes to avoid

Posting products without prices

This creates unnecessary back-and-forth and can discourage potential buyers.

Using vague product descriptions

"Beautiful handmade basket" tells the customer very little.

Underpricing labour

If you never pay yourself for your time, the business may look profitable while actually consuming your labour for free.

Accepting orders without recording them

Memory is a terrible inventory-management system.

Promising delivery everywhere

Test delivery routes before making broad promises.

Mixing personal and business money

You need to know whether the business is making money.

Relying entirely on one social-media account

Keep your catalogue, photographs and customer records somewhere you control.

Ignoring intellectual property

Your photographs and original designs have value.

Treating every follower as a customer

A smaller audience that actually buys can be more useful than thousands of passive followers.

The bottom line

For Uganda's craft makers, e-commerce does not have to mean abandoning markets, shops or physical exhibitions.

It can simply add another route to the customer.

A seller can meet someone at a craft market, gain a WhatsApp contact, make a sale later, deliver the product and eventually turn that customer into a repeat buyer. A tourist can discover a product during a visit and order another one months later. A Kampala customer can buy from a maker in another district without travelling to the workshop.

The technology is only one part of that process.

The real competitive advantage comes from getting the ordinary things right: good products, honest photographs, sensible pricing, accurate descriptions, reliable payment, careful packaging, dependable delivery and prompt communication.

Uganda's digital audience gives handmade businesses more opportunities to be discovered. Whether those opportunities become sustainable income depends on what happens after the customer sends the first message.
