How to Check If an Online Job Offer Is Genuine Before you Apply

Learn how to verify online job offers, spot recruitment scams, avoid fake fees and check overseas recruitment agencies in Uganda.

SSEGUPost2026-10-21

 

The message usually arrives on WhatsApp. It carries a company logo, a formal greeting and an offer that seems almost too good to ignore. Perhaps the job is in Dubai, with accommodation included. Perhaps it is a remote position paying in dollars, or an administrative role with a company whose name you recognise. The recruiter sounds professional and says your application has been successful.

But then they ask you to pay a registration fee, send a copy of your National ID, deposit money for equipment or settle a visa-processing charge before the appointment can be confirmed.


For someone who has spent weeks looking for work, the temptation to proceed is understandable.  I know, i've been there.  A job offer is not merely an email or a document. It represents rent paid on time, school fees for a child, money sent home or the possibility of escaping an exhausting financial situation. Scammers understand that urgency, and their schemes are designed to exploit it.

The difficulty is that legitimate employers recruit online every day. Genuine vacancies circulate through WhatsApp groups, Facebook pages, recruitment agencies and job websites. Some legitimate recruiters use personal email addresses or conduct interviews over video calls. A suspicious-looking detail does not necessarily prove fraud, just as a polished appointment letter does not prove that a job exists.


The most reliable way to assess an online job offer is to establish whether the employer, the vacancy and the person making the offer can be verified independently.

A familiar company name is not proof of a genuine job

One of the easiest mistakes is to concentrate on the company rather than the person claiming to represent it.

Suppose a recruiter contacts you about a position at a well-known international company. The company exists, its website looks professional and the offer letter carries its logo. You search the recruiter's name on LinkedIn and find a profile with the correct job title.


Everything appears to fit. Yet none of those details establishes that the person contacting you works for the company.

Company logos, employee photographs, signatures and employment documents can be copied. Fraudsters can create websites that resemble legitimate corporate pages and email addresses that differ from genuine ones by a single character. A real employee's name can also be used without their knowledge.


The sensible next step is to leave the conversation and approach the company independently. Type its known website address into your browser, locate the careers page or official contact information, and ask whether the position and recruiter are genuine. If the company publishes a recruitment telephone number or human resources email address, use that rather than the contact details supplied in the suspicious message.

A useful question is straightforward: Can you confirm that this vacancy exists and that this person is authorised to recruit for it?


If the company cannot confirm the details, the offer remains unverified. A recruiter who insists that contacting the employer will jeopardise your application has given you another reason to pause.

This distinction matters because searching for the company itself is not enough. You need to verify the particular recruitment process through a channel the suspected recruiter does not control.


The recruitment process should make sense

A genuine job offer usually follows a process that can be explained. The employer should be able to tell you what the work involves, who you would report to, where the job is based, what the remuneration arrangements are and what happens before you start.

That process does not have to be lengthy. Some employers recruit quickly, and certain casual or entry-level positions require only a short interview. A remote employer may never meet an applicant in person. These arrangements are not automatically suspicious.


The concern arises when the offer has no credible connection to your qualifications or experience, or when basic questions produce evasive answers.

Imagine receiving an offer for a well-paid administrative position after submitting no application, attending no interview and providing no information about your skills. The recruiter says hundreds of people applied but you were selected immediately. Before you can ask about the duties, you are instructed to pay for onboarding.


The salary may be attractive, but the sequence is difficult to explain as normal recruitment.

Check the vacancy against the employer's official careers page. Compare the job title, responsibilities, location, qualifications and any published salary range. If the vacancy is absent, ask the employer to confirm it directly; not every legitimate position is advertised publicly.


A video interview is useful evidence, but it is not conclusive. Scammers can conduct interviews, send employment contracts and create onboarding portals. What matters is whether the employer can independently confirm the person conducting the interview and the position being offered.

The same caution applies to unusually generous remote-work offers. A promise of substantial daily earnings for liking videos, rating products or completing simple tasks deserves particular scrutiny when the recruiter provides little information about the business and asks you to deposit money before withdrawing your supposed earnings.


When getting the job requires paying for it

The request for money is often the point at which a questionable offer becomes a serious financial risk.

The explanation varies. One recruiter needs a registration fee. Another says applicants must pay for compulsory training, a background check, a laptop, a work permit or a visa. A third promises that every expense will be refunded with the first salary.


Some employment-related costs are legitimate in particular circumstances, and the rules differ by country and type of work. The important question is not simply whether a fee exists, but whether the charge is lawful, properly explained and independently verifiable.

A particularly common remote-work scenario involves equipment. The supposed employer tells a successful applicant to buy a laptop from a designated supplier, promising reimbursement after the employee starts. The applicant pays, but the supplier never delivers the equipment and the recruiter disappears.


Another variation involves a cheque. The applicant receives what appears to be a payment for salary or equipment, then is instructed to send part of the money to a supplier. The cheque later proves fraudulent or the payment is reversed, leaving the applicant responsible for the money transferred.

There is also a distinction between a genuine task-based job and a task scam. In the latter, an online platform displays supposed earnings for simple activities but requires deposits to unlock further tasks or withdraw the balance. The figures shown on the screen may have no relationship to money actually available to the worker.


The US Federal Trade Commission has warned about these kinds of employment scams, including fake equipment purchases, fraudulent cheques and schemes that require workers to pay their own money.


For Ugandan job seekers, mobile money adds another practical concern. A recruiter may ask for a payment to a personal number, perhaps describing it as a refundable booking fee or a final step before an appointment letter is released. A mobile-money transaction receipt proves that money was transferred; it does not prove that the recipient is a legitimate recruitment agent.

Before paying any employment-related charge, establish its basis with an independent, reliable source. If the explanation depends entirely on what the recruiter tells you, the verification is incomplete.


Never deposit a cheque and forward part of the proceeds to someone you have met only through an unverified recruitment process. And if a supposed employer requires a deposit before allowing you to withdraw your online earnings, do not send more money in an attempt to recover what the platform claims you have already earned.


The employment contract deserves equal attention. Before committing to an overseas position, establish the legal identity of the employer, the destination and workplace, the salary, working hours, accommodation arrangements, deductions and other relevant conditions. Understand what the contract says about travel, termination and the responsibilities of each party.


Be particularly cautious when someone promises guaranteed travel or immediate employment in exchange for a payment, while avoiding questions about the employer or the necessary immigration arrangements.

An agency may be genuine while a particular offer is not. An employer may be real while an impersonator uses its name. Each part of the arrangement needs to be checked.


Your identity documents are not harmless application attachments

Recruitment involves personal information. An employer may reasonably need a CV, qualifications, identification and, at the appropriate stage, bank details for payroll. Overseas employment can also require passport information and other documents.


The danger lies in disclosing information before you know who is collecting it, why it is needed and how it will be handled.

An unsolicited recruiter asking for your National ID before explaining the position deserves scrutiny. So does a supposed employer that requests your banking password or mobile-money PIN to activate a payroll account.


A legitimate salary payment does not require you to disclose your banking password, card security code or one-time verification code to a recruiter. These details should remain private regardless of how convincing the offer appears.


If an employer genuinely needs an identity document, ask why it is required at that stage and whether there is a secure submission method. Where appropriate, use a redacted copy for preliminary checks, provided the employer accepts it and the redactions do not defeat the stated purpose. Do not alter official documents deceptively or assume that redaction makes every disclosure safe.


Be wary of forms that request more information than the recruitment process reasonably requires. A convincing website can still be designed to harvest identity documents, account details and other sensitive information.

For questions about the misuse of personal data in Uganda, the Personal Data Protection Office provides information on data protection and complaints.


What to do if the offer turns out to be fraudulent

People sometimes recognise a scam only after sending money or documents. That does not mean they should continue engaging with the recruiter in the hope of fixing the situation.


If you have transferred money, contact your bank, mobile-money provider or payment service immediately. Explain that the transaction may be fraudulent and ask whether it can be stopped, traced or reversed. Recovery is not guaranteed, but prompt reporting gives the provider an opportunity to advise you on the available options.


Preserve the evidence before blocking the sender. Save the messages, email addresses, telephone numbers, website links, offer letters, payment instructions and transaction references. These details may help the payment provider, platform or police investigate the incident.


If you shared a password or verification code, change exposed credentials immediately. Start with your email account and any financial accounts that may be affected, and change reused passwords elsewhere. Contact your financial provider if an account may have been compromised.


If you sent your National ID or passport, keep a record of what you disclosed and watch for suspicious activity or attempts to impersonate you. Seek guidance from the relevant authority if you believe the document may be misused.


Report suspected fraud to the appropriate police authorities and to the platform where the offer appeared. If the scammer impersonated a real company or recruitment agency, notify that organisation through its official channels.


One further warning is worth taking seriously: people who have already lost money may be targeted again by supposed recovery agents promising to retrieve it for a fee. Do not make another payment simply because someone claims to have a special route to recovering the first one.


The test is whether the offer survives independent scrutiny

Before accepting an online job, take a moment to establish who is hiring, whether the vacancy exists, what the work entails and whether the recruitment process makes sense. Check contact details independently. Verify overseas recruitment agencies through EEMIS where applicable. Question requests for money and disclose personal information only when there is a legitimate, verified reason.


Searches for the company name, recruiter and suspicious phrases can uncover complaints or warnings, but the absence of negative search results does not establish that an offer is safe. Nor does a polished contract, a professional interview or a convincing social media profile.

The Federal Trade Commission's job scam guidance is a useful additional resource, particularly for applicants considering remote work with foreign employers.


For anyone looking for work in Uganda, caution should not mean dismissing every opportunity that arrives online. Digital recruitment has made it easier to discover vacancies that would otherwise be difficult to find. The aim is to distinguish a real opportunity from an impersonation before the cost of finding out becomes too high.


A genuine employer should be able to withstand reasonable questions about its identity, vacancy and recruitment process. If the person making the offer insists that you must pay or surrender sensitive information before you can verify those details, the sensible response is to stop and investigate.


An offer is not genuine because it looks official. It is genuine when the employer, the job and the person recruiting you can be independently verified.
