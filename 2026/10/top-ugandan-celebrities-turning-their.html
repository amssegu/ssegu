Beyond the Stage: 10 Ugandan Celebrities Who Turned Fame Into Business

Meet 10 Ugandan celebrities who have moved beyond entertainment into business

SSEGUPost2026-10-09

 
Fame can open a door. It does not, by itself, pay the rent on the building behind it.

For Ugandan musicians, comedians and other public figures, the move from entertainment into business is often presented as a simple progression: become famous, build a following, launch a company. In reality, the transition is considerably messier.


A musician can have millions of views and still struggle with cash flow. A popular performer can fill a concert and discover that running a restaurant is an entirely different proposition. A celebrity can put a name on a product and still have to persuade customers that the product itself is worth buying.


That is what makes Uganda's celebrity entrepreneurs interesting.

Some have built businesses closely connected to entertainment. Others have moved into hospitality, fashion, food, consumer products or creative services. Some ventures have lasted; others have been abandoned, reshaped or struggled.


There is also a necessary caveat. Private businesses rarely publish audited accounts, while the internet is remarkably fond of inventing celebrity net worth figures. This is therefore not a list of Uganda's richest celebrities, nor a ranking of who has made the most money.


It is a look at publicly documented businesses and commercial ventures associated with Ugandan entertainers and public personalities, and what their stories reveal about the rather complicated business of turning fame into something that can survive beyond the spotlight.


Eddy Kenzo and the business behind the artist

Eddy Kenzo's career offers one of the clearest examples of a Ugandan entertainer trying to build something larger than an individual music career.

The singer is associated with Big Talent Entertainment, a platform involved in music and talent development. He has also been involved in nurturing young talent, including the Big Talent Soccer Academy, launched in Kampala in 2019. Kenzo is also the president and founder of the Uganda National Musicians Federation.


The significance of Big Talent is less about the name on the building than the idea behind it.

There is a difference between being the person who performs the music and building an organisation around music. The latter can involve artists, recordings, management, performances, intellectual property and commercial partnerships, allowing the business to generate value from more than one person's appearances.


That is a difficult transition for entertainers because it introduces a less glamorous side of the industry: contracts, revenue splits, ownership, administration and the long-running question of who actually owns what. A label is not simply a group of artists wearing the same logo.


Bebe Cool and the formalisation of Gagamel

Bebe Cool has spent years developing Gagamel into a recognisable brand around his music career.

In 2014, he opened Gagamel offices in Kololo, describing the premises as a base for bookings, music sales and promotion, with plans for production facilities.


It was a relatively unglamorous development compared with a concert or music video, but perhaps a more consequential one. The office represented an attempt to put an organisation around the celebrity.

That distinction matters in entertainment, where much of the business can otherwise remain informal. Bookings, promotions, merchandise and relationships with clients all become easier to manage when there is an actual operation behind the public figure.


Bebe Cool has also been associated with Gagamel merchandise and other commercial activities. More recently, reports have attributed a stone-quarry business to him, although the private venture is considerably less documented publicly than his entertainment activities.


The broader point is not that a celebrity office or another investment proves commercial success. It does not.

It simply shows the continuing effort to turn a music identity into something with a wider commercial footprint.


Sheebah Karungi's move from music into products and experiences

Sheebah Karungi has taken a particularly consumer-facing approach to business.

Her ventures have included Holic Pads, a sanitary-products brand launched in 2020, alongside hair and fashion-related businesses and event activities. Her company was reported as Sheebah Establishments Company, under which Holic Pads was launched.


She also experimented with turning her audience into customers of an experience rather than a physical product. In 2021, she launched the Queen Karma Cruise, with bookings handled through Red Events.

It is an interesting evolution because the products are not simply extensions of recorded music. They sit in categories where her public identity, audience and interests can intersect with ordinary consumer demand.


That is one of the more difficult things for a celebrity business to get right. A famous name can generate curiosity, but curiosity is not necessarily repeat business.

The Holic Pads story also demonstrates why celebrity partnerships deserve a little more scrutiny than social media announcements usually provide. Media reports later described difficulties surrounding the partnership, although those reports should not be treated as audited evidence of the company's financial performance.


Behind the publicity, a business partnership still needs the same things as any other: clearly defined ownership, responsibilities and financial arrangements.


Julius Kyazze built the business behind the music

Julius Kyazze is perhaps one of the more revealing examples because his work has often happened behind the artists who receive the attention.


Kyazze co-founded Swangz Avenue with Benon Mugumbya. What began around music production grew into a wider creative operation involving artist management, commercial production, advertising, events and communications.


In 2024, Kyazze said the record-label side accounted for about 15% of Swangz Avenue's revenue, with the company increasingly earning from communication materials, advertising, marketing, public relations and related commercial work.

That evolution says something important about the economics of creative businesses.


The same cameras, editors, producers, designers and production capabilities that can make a music video can also be sold to a corporate client. Once that becomes possible, the company is no longer dependent entirely on whether an artist has a hit song.

It becomes a services business.


There is another lesson in the Kyazze-Mugumbya partnership. Creative businesses often need people with different strengths. The person who can produce the work is not necessarily the person who should negotiate the contract, manage the finances or build the commercial side.

Sometimes the most valuable business partner is simply the person who is good at the things you are not.


Zuena Kirema found a business beyond the celebrity spotlight

Zuena Kirema's story is rather different because her business is not an extension of performing at all.

She founded Zuena Events and Cakes, combining baking with event management and decoration.

In an interview with HiPipo Money, Kirema described starting the cake business on a relatively small scale, initially investing about UGX 3 million and reinvesting savings from sales. She later said her husband, Bebe Cool, helped her acquire a commercial oven costing about UGX 25 million.


Those are historical figures from her account, rather than estimates of what it would cost to start a similar business today.

What makes her story stand out is its ordinariness.

There was no need to invent a new category around celebrity. Cakes already had customers. Weddings, birthdays, graduations and corporate events already existed. The business was built around serving that demand better and at a larger scale.

It is a reminder that entrepreneurship does not always begin with a revolutionary idea. Sometimes it begins with noticing that people are already willing to pay for something you can make.


Anne Kansiime took her audience into hospitality

Anne Kansiime built her public profile through comedy and acting, but one of her businesses operates far away from the stage.


Kansiime Backpackers, in the Lake Bunyonyi area of Kabale, takes the comedian's brand into tourism and hospitality. Her official website identifies the property as one of her businesses and lists accommodation and services including Wi-Fi, reception and laundry.

Hospitality is an especially interesting choice for someone coming from entertainment because the two industries operate on very different rhythms.


A comedian can earn from performances, appearances, content and partnerships.

A hotel or backpackers' property has to deal with occupancy, cleaning, staff, maintenance, utilities, food, pricing and customer service whether the owner is famous that week or not.

The celebrity connection may help a property get noticed. After that, the ordinary rules of hospitality take over.


A famous name can persuade someone to make a booking.

It cannot persuade them to return after a poor stay.


Zari Hassan and the business of the personal brand

Zari Hassan's commercial identity extends well beyond entertainment.

Her business interests have included education, property, lifestyle, beauty, entertainment and brand partnerships, much of it based outside Uganda, particularly in South Africa.

That geographical distinction matters. Zari is a Ugandan public personality whose business career is international; she is not simply an example of a celebrity running businesses in Uganda.


A 2026 profile by Daily Monitor described her evolution from socialite and reality-TV personality into a businesswoman and brand builder, including her interest in developing assets and systems that are not completely dependent on her physical presence.

That is perhaps the most interesting part of the story. There is a substantial difference between monetising attention and owning an asset.

The former depends heavily on remaining visible, the latter can potentially retain value even when the founder is not standing in front of a camera.

Property, a school, a product company, a brand or intellectual property all operate on a different logic from simply being famous.


King Saha's experiment with hospitality

King Saha has also looked beyond music.

In 2020, the musician launched King's Spot, a restaurant and bar near Nkumba University in Entebbe. Contemporary reporting described the venue as offering food, barbecue, drinks and space for private functions. Saha has also been associated in reporting with farming and real-estate investments.


Restaurants and entertainment have an obvious attraction for musicians. The networks overlap. Artists know DJs, MCs, promoters, photographers and event organisers. A musician can potentially bring some of that network into a new venue.


But hospitality has little patience for celebrity.

Rent arrives whether customers do or not. Food spoils. Staff need salaries. Utilities continue running. Licences and taxes do not disappear because the owner has a large following.

The result is a familiar paradox in celebrity entrepreneurship: the audience can provide an excellent starting advantage, but the business still has to work on its own terms.


Jose Chameleone's more complicated business story

Jose Chameleone's business history is useful precisely because it does not read like a succession of effortless victories.

His Leone Island music label became an important part of his career, with several Ugandan artists passing through it over the years.

He also experimented outside music.


One example was Chameleone Mobile, a branded mobile-phone venture. Contemporary reporting said the phones struggled to gain traction in Uganda, with complaints from customers about battery performance and duplication.

He later entered hospitality with DNA Lounge in Kololo. By 2019, reporting said the lounge had experienced losses and difficulties involving rent and operational taxes, after which Chameleone sought to sell the business.


There is value in including these less polished chapters in any discussion of celebrity entrepreneurship.

Business failure is not an unusual phenomenon that happens only to people without famous names.

Sometimes an investment simply does not work.

The ability to recognise that early enough to stop further losses may be just as important as the confidence required to start in the first place.


Flex D'Paper and the commercial life of a rap identity

For rapper Flex D'Paper, the route into business began with something considerably smaller than a major corporate venture.

His Rapaholix identity expanded from music into clothing. In an interview with The Tribe UG, Flex explained that the idea began with a cap and T-shirt made for himself. Other people wanted the items, and the possibility of a clothing business emerged from there.


It is an unusually clean example of a basic business principle: sometimes the market tells you what to sell.

The rapper's audience already had an interest in the identity surrounding his music. Clothing provided a way to make that identity physical.


But fashion has a way of bringing an entrepreneur back to earth. Customers still care about fabric, stitching, sizing, design, finishing and price. A popular artist can generate the first wave of attention; that does not automatically produce a viable clothing company.

For any small Ugandan fashion label, the question eventually becomes less about who is wearing the clothes and more about whether customers who bought once want to buy again.


What happens when fame meets business?

Put these stories next to one another and the differences are more interesting than the similarities.

Eddy Kenzo built around talent.

Julius Kyazze built a creative-services company.

Zuena Kirema built around cakes and events.

Anne Kansiime moved into hospitality.

Flex D'Paper turned a music identity into merchandise.

Sheebah Karungi explored consumer products and experiences.

Zari Hassan developed a broader lifestyle and investment-oriented personal brand.

Others, including Bebe Cool, King Saha and Jose Chameleone, have experimented with several commercial directions alongside their music careers.

There is no single celebrity-business formula here. What they do share is access to something most new businesses spend years trying to acquire: attention.

The difficult part is converting that attention into something more durable.

An Instagram follower is not necessarily a customer. A viral song is not necessarily a business. A sold-out concert does not necessarily mean the artist understands hospitality. And a successful artist does not automatically become a successful retailer, restaurant owner or manufacturer.

The business has to develop its own logic. That means knowing what is being sold, who is buying it, what it costs to produce and deliver, and whether there is anything left after the bills are paid.


The part of the story social media rarely shows

Celebrity business announcements tend to arrive fully formed.

A photograph. A logo. A new shop. A ribbon-cutting ceremony. A caption about a "new chapter".

What remains outside the frame is usually less photogenic.

There are suppliers who want payment. Employees who need salaries. Customers who complain. Stock that does not sell. Equipment that breaks. Partners who disagree. Tax obligations. Rent. Loans. Failed experiments.

And sometimes, after all of that, there is a business that simply does not work.

That is why the more useful question is not How rich is this celebrity?

It is:

What have they actually built?

A music catalogue can be an asset. So can a production company, clothing brand, hospitality business, school, customer base, intellectual property or trained team.

Those things can potentially survive changes in public attention.

Fame itself is more fragile.

Fame is the head start, not the business

The most revealing thing about Uganda's celebrity entrepreneurs may be that fame is both their greatest advantage and one of their biggest distractions.

It can bring customers through the door. It can attract investors, media coverage and partnerships. it can make a new product visible overnight.


But once the attention fades, the underlying business remains.

Customers still expect quality. Staff still need management. Suppliers still want payment. Taxes still have to be dealt with. A restaurant still needs customers on Tuesday afternoon. A clothing brand still needs people to like the clothes after the celebrity photograph has disappeared from Instagram.


That is where the distinction between being famous and building a business becomes clear.

The first can get people to notice you, the second has to give them a reason to stay.

And perhaps that is the most useful part of these stories. The interesting question is not which celebrity looks the most successful from the outside.

It is what remains after the spotlight moves elsewhere.
