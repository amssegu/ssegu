10 Things You Should Never Put on Your CV

10 things to leave off your CV, from sensitive personal information to false claims, and learn how to protect your privacy when applying for jobs.

SSEGUPost2026-10-14

 


A job application asks a stranger to take you seriously. Your CV is often the first evidence they have of your experience, judgement and ability to do the work. Before you have spoken to a recruiter or sat across from an interviewer, a few pages of text must make the case for you.

That can tempt applicants to include everything they have accumulated: every qualification, every personal detail, every skill they can vaguely claim and every impressive number they think might catch someone's attention. Others attach copies of their identification documents, list referees without asking permission or send the same elaborate CV to every employer they encounter.

Some of these habits make an application less convincing. Others expose information that should not be circulating among strangers.

For jobseekers in Uganda, the distinction matters beyond the office. An application might go to a local company, a recruitment agency advertising domestic work in the Gulf, a factory hiring workers overseas or someone who has copied a genuine vacancy to collect applicants' personal information. The document that introduces you to an employer can also become a record of information you never needed to disclose.

The best CV is not necessarily the one with the most information. It is the one that gives an employer enough credible evidence to make a decision, without creating unnecessary risks along the way.

When a CV becomes a liability

Consider the personal information many applicants include almost automatically. A National Identification Number, passport number or bank account detail might appear useful because it establishes identity. In reality, a general CV rarely needs any of them. Your name, telephone number, professional email address and relevant location are normally enough to start a conversation.

The distinction becomes important when applying online. A recruiter may eventually need to verify your identity, but that does not mean an unfamiliar person needs a copy of your passport before explaining the job. Canada's official Job Bank advises jobseekers to protect sensitive identification and financial information during recruitment. Its guidance is useful for Ugandans applying online, even though the rules and procedures of individual employers and countries differ.

Never include mobile money PINs, banking passwords or one-time verification codes in a CV or share them to secure an interview. Those are credentials for accessing accounts, not evidence of professional suitability.

Identity documents require a more considered approach. An employer, licensed recruitment agency or immigration authority may legitimately request them at a particular stage. The sensible response is to establish who is asking, why the documents are needed and how they will be submitted securely. Privacy does not mean refusing every request; it means understanding the request before complying.

This is especially important when pursuing work abroad. A convincing advert, a polished website or a recruiter communicating through WhatsApp does not, by itself, establish that an opportunity is genuine. Uganda's Ministry of Gender, Labour and Social Development provides an official starting point for information about external recruitment and labour export: mglsd.go.ug.

The personal details that can work against you

A CV is a professional introduction, not a biography. Your marital status, religion, political views and unrelated health information generally have little place in an application unless a specific, legitimate requirement makes them relevant.

Age, date of birth and nationality are also unnecessary on many standard CVs. The UK's National Careers Service advises applicants not to include age, date of birth, marital status or nationality on a standard CV. Canada's Job Bank similarly discourages unnecessary personal information.

These recommendations reflect the conventions of those destinations, not a universal rule governing every country or vacancy. Employers may need to establish work authorisation, eligibility or other relevant details through an application form or formal verification process. Some local conventions also differ.

For someone applying from Uganda to the UK, Canada or another foreign destination, the practical lesson is to follow the requirements of the actual vacancy and country rather than assuming that every CV should look the same.

Photographs fall into the same category. Some applicants attach a photograph because they believe it makes the application more complete. In Canada, photographs are not the norm on résumés, and the Job Bank advises against including one. Other countries and particular industries may follow different conventions.

Unless the employer requests a photograph or the destination's established practice calls for one, leaving it out is usually the more straightforward choice. The space is better used for relevant experience.

The trouble with making yourself look more qualified than you are

There is another kind of exposure that has nothing to do with identity theft: the professional risk of claiming too much.

A candidate might describe themselves as an advanced Excel user after learning a few basic formulas, claim to have supervised a team they merely worked alongside or list a qualification they have not completed. A CV can make these claims look impressive, but an interview can quickly reveal the difference between familiarity and competence.

The same problem appears in inflated achievements. Statements such as “increased company profits by 80 per cent” or “improved efficiency by 95 per cent” sound persuasive until someone asks how the figures were calculated, what period they cover and what the applicant personally contributed.

Numbers are valuable when they describe something real. They become decoration when the person presenting them cannot explain where they came from.

A storekeeper, for example, might write that they recorded incoming stock, maintained inventory records and assisted with weekly stock counts. That description may seem less dramatic than claiming to have managed an entire warehouse, but it tells an employer what the candidate actually knows how to do.

Likewise, a web developer can describe a website they built, the technologies used and the problems they solved. A customer service applicant can explain the types of enquiries handled and the systems used to resolve them. Neither needs invented percentages to make the experience worthwhile.

This matters particularly to graduates and applicants changing careers, who sometimes feel that a short employment history leaves them with little to say. Relevant projects, practical training, volunteering and genuine technical skills are better evidence than exaggerated job titles or fabricated achievements.

Your previous employer's business is not yours to disclose

A CV should demonstrate professional experience without revealing information entrusted to you by someone else.

Customer records, private account details, internal financial reports and commercially sensitive procedures can all cross the line between describing your work and disclosing confidential information. This is a concern for people who have worked in banking, healthcare, customer service, software development and other fields involving private or proprietary information.

The alternative is not to make your experience so vague that it becomes meaningless. It is to describe your responsibilities at the appropriate level.

Someone who prepared reports for business clients can say so without naming the clients or publishing their confidential figures. An employee who handled sensitive records can explain their experience with record-keeping, access controls or established security procedures without revealing the procedures themselves.

The ability to distinguish between experience worth sharing and information that must remain private is part of professional judgement. A prospective employer has little reason to trust someone who demonstrates their expertise by exposing a former employer's confidential material.

The quieter mistakes: salary demands, referees and irrelevant history

Some CV mistakes are less dramatic but still weaken an application.

Salary expectations are one example. A fixed demand written into a CV can narrow a negotiation before an employer has considered the applicant's experience. Previous salary details may also be irrelevant to the new position. Unless the vacancy requests this information, it is generally better to address compensation separately.

For Ugandans considering overseas work, salary figures deserve particular care. A monthly salary quoted in another currency tells only part of the story. Working hours, accommodation, transport, deductions, benefits and the cost of living all affect what an offer is worth. Before accepting a position, establish whether the advertised amount is gross or net, which currency will be used and what the contract actually provides.

Referees require similar consideration. A former supervisor or lecturer may be happy to recommend you, but that does not mean their telephone number should circulate among every organisation receiving your CV. Ask permission before sharing their details, and give them enough information to understand the positions for which they may be contacted. If references are not requested, there is no need to include their private contact information merely to make the document look complete.

Then there is the temptation to fill space. Old short courses, unrelated hobbies, lengthy childhood histories and lists of flattering adjectives can push useful evidence out of sight. Calling yourself a “hard-working, honest and highly motivated team player” tells an employer little without an example of what you have accomplished.

A CV does not need to be packed to look substantial. A recent graduate can use a relevant project to demonstrate practical ability. An experienced machine operator can highlight equipment handled, safety procedures followed and responsibilities undertaken. An applicant for an administrative role can focus on record-keeping, scheduling and communication.

The test is relevance, not whether a detail sounds impressive in isolation. Keep an older qualification or hobby when it contributes something useful; remove it when it merely occupies space.

Why clever CV tricks are rarely clever

The final temptation is to treat recruitment software as an obstacle to outwit. Applicants sometimes hide keywords in white text, repeat phrases from the job advert unnaturally or use elaborate graphics to make their skills look more impressive.

These techniques confuse presentation with evidence. Hidden text can produce an awkward result when a document is extracted into plain text, while percentage bars claiming that someone is “90 per cent proficient” in Excel or Python communicate very little about what that person can actually do.

A simpler CV is often easier to assess. Use clear headings, readable text and a layout that keeps employment history, qualifications and skills easy to locate. Include relevant terminology from the vacancy, but only where it accurately describes your experience.

Follow the employer's instructions about file format. A text-based PDF is a reasonable choice when no other format is specified, although some employers may request a Word document or require applicants to complete an online form.

The point is not to design the most elaborate document. It is to make the evidence of your suitability clear, accurate and easy to find.

What a recruiter should learn from your CV

Removing unnecessary details is only half the job. A strong CV still needs to establish who you are professionally and why your application deserves attention.

Your name and contact details should be easy to find. Your education, qualifications and work history should be accurate. The skills section should reflect abilities relevant to the position, while projects, volunteering and practical training can help demonstrate competence when formal employment experience is limited.

A short professional summary can help explain your background, but it should not become a collection of adjectives. The strongest descriptions tell the reader what you do, what you know and what sort of contribution you can make.

Above all, tailor the document to the vacancy. A factory operator, a customer service representative and a junior software developer should not present precisely the same evidence in precisely the same order. Their experience may overlap, but employers are looking for different things.

The UK's National Careers Service offers guidance on CV sections and presentation at nationalcareers.service.gov.uk. Canada's Job Bank provides a separate guide to writing a résumé at jobbank.gc.ca. Both are useful starting points, provided applicants adapt the advice to the destination and the vacancy.

Before sending an application, read the CV once as a recruiter might: quickly, looking for relevant experience and reasons to trust the claims being made. Then read it again for information that does not belong there, including sensitive identification details, unsubstantiated achievements and other people's private contact information.

A CV should open a door, not tell your entire story or hand over information that nobody needs yet. The strongest applications are selective without being evasive, confident without exaggeration, and informative without compromising privacy.
