How to Get a UAE Work Visa from Uganda: A Guide to Labour Export Agencies, Requirements and Costs

how Ugandans can get a UAE work visa, verify job offers, prepare documents, understand visa costs and avoid recruitment scams.

SSEGUPost2026-10-12

For many Ugandans hoping to work in the United Arab Emirates, the journey does not begin with an application to a hotel, security company or private household in Dubai. It begins at a labour recruitment office in Kampala, with a vacancy advertised on social media, or with someone who knows an agent recruiting workers for the Gulf.

The jobs vary. Some are for housemaids, nannies, cleaners and other domestic workers. Others involve security, warehouse operations, hospitality, construction and general support services. An applicant may be interviewed, undergo screening or training, submit documents and wait for deployment while the recruitment agency coordinates the process with an employer abroad.

This route is important because applicants do not always have the contacts, resources or experience to approach overseas employers directly. A labour-export company can connect a Ugandan worker to a vacancy, coordinate documentation and recruitment formalities, and help arrange travel. For someone seeking their first job abroad, that assistance can make the process more accessible.

But it also introduces another party whose promises must be checked.

A legitimate Ugandan recruitment agency can help arrange overseas employment; it cannot make an unapproved job legitimate simply by promising to handle everything. The worker still needs to understand the employment terms, the applicable visa process, the costs involved and the protections available if something goes wrong.

For Ugandans seeking work in the UAE, the safest approach is to understand how the recruitment system works before handing over money, surrendering documents or committing to travel.

The labour-export route: how most applicants encounter overseas jobs

There are two broad ways to pursue employment in the UAE. You can apply directly to an overseas employer, or you can go through a recruitment company in Uganda that sources vacancies and coordinates the recruitment process.

Under the agency route, the company may advertise an approved vacancy, receive applications, screen candidates, arrange interviews, coordinate required documentation and communicate with the overseas employer. Depending on the job, it may also organise pre-departure preparation and provide instructions about travel and arrival.

The exact arrangements differ between agencies and employment categories. Some vacancies involve a straightforward recruitment process; others require additional screening, training, medical checks or professional approvals.

Uganda's Ministry of Gender, Labour and Social Development oversees the country's external employment recruitment system. Its External Employment Management Information System (EEMIS) allows prospective workers to browse overseas vacancies and check recruitment companies.

The range of advertised jobs illustrates why this system matters. The portal lists domestic-worker vacancies alongside opportunities in areas such as security, cleaning, warehouse work and other support services. The terms, salaries and destination countries vary by vacancy.

Rather than beginning with a random social media advert, an applicant can use EEMIS to identify a recruitment company and a specific vacancy, then establish whether both are currently authorised.

This is not merely a formality. A recruitment company's registration and a job's approval are separate matters worth checking. An agency may be genuine while a person falsely claiming to represent it circulates a fabricated vacancy. A real employer's name can also be used in an unauthorised advert.

The Ministry's External Employment information page explains the government's role in regulating labour migration from Uganda.

Choosing an agency is the first major decision

For applicants pursuing jobs as domestic workers, security guards or cleaners, the recruitment agency may be the main source of information about the employer and the process. That makes it important to establish who is responsible for each part of the arrangement.

Start by checking the company's name on EEMIS. Confirm its current authorisation, recorded contact details and address. Then locate the particular vacancy and compare its advertised terms with what the recruiter has told you.

If the recruiter promises a job that does not appear in the information supplied, ask for clarification before proceeding. The absence of a listing should prompt verification with the Ministry rather than an automatic conclusion that the offer is fraudulent.

Use independently verified contact details when speaking to the company. If someone approaches you through WhatsApp claiming to represent a licensed agency, contact the agency through its official details to establish whether the person works there and whether the vacancy is genuine.

A proper recruitment process should give you enough information to understand the position before you commit. You should know the employer or the authorised recruitment arrangement, the job category, the destination, the salary, the contract period and the conditions under which you will work.

Be particularly careful when an agent asks for money before identifying the employer or providing any written documentation. A claim that the vacancy is almost filled is not a substitute for verification.

The important distinction is between a company that coordinates a documented recruitment process and an intermediary who relies on urgency, verbal promises and unexplained payments.

The process depends on the job you are taking

Not every person travelling from Uganda to the UAE for employment follows the same immigration or labour procedure. The type of work matters.

Domestic workers: housemaids, nannies and home managers

Domestic work includes roles such as housemaids, nannies, cooks, household helpers and other workers employed in private households. These jobs are subject to a separate UAE legal framework governing domestic workers, including Federal Decree-Law No. 9 of 2022 and its amendments.

In this arrangement, the recruitment process may involve a Ugandan agency working with an overseas domestic-worker recruitment office or another authorised party. The worker may not deal directly with the household employing them until later in the process.

The agency should explain the actual job, the employer's requirements, the salary, the contract period, the living arrangements and the relevant employment and immigration procedures.

For live-in domestic work, accommodation may be provided in the employer's home. That arrangement makes it especially important to understand the conditions before departure: where you will sleep, what duties you will perform, how rest days are handled and how you can contact someone outside the household if you need assistance.

The UAE's official domestic-worker guidance outlines the applicable legal framework and worker protections. The rules prohibit practices including forced labour, harassment and assigning duties outside the scope of the contract.

Domestic-worker recruitment arrangements also have specific rules concerning fees and commissions. UAE law prohibits domestic-worker recruitment offices from demanding or receiving commissions or financial rewards from workers in exchange for employment. The employer-facing recruitment arrangement is therefore not a reason for a worker to accept an unexplained commission charge.

Security guards, cleaners and warehouse workers

Security guards, cleaners and warehouse workers may be recruited through Ugandan labour-export companies, but they should not automatically be treated as domestic workers.

A security guard employed by a security company, for example, generally follows the applicable private-sector employment and work-permit route, together with any occupation-specific approvals required by the relevant authority. Domestic-worker rules should not be assumed to apply merely because the job was arranged through a labour-export agency.

The offer should identify the actual employer and workplace. A position described as security work should not quietly become a different job after arrival, and a promised salary should not be replaced by a lower amount in the final contract without the issue being addressed.

Some security positions may require particular qualifications, training or local licensing. Ask the agency whether these are necessary for the specific vacancy and who will arrange them.

Other jobs

Hospitality, construction, driving, technical work and professional employment may involve different documentation and approval requirements.

The recruitment agency should identify the correct employment category rather than offering one generic explanation of the visa process for every job. The UAE government's guidance on employing expatriates in private-sector companies provides the general framework for the standard private-sector route.

What the recruitment agency should arrange

One reason applicants use labour-export companies is that the process can involve several organisations and stages. Depending on the vacancy and the agency's role, the company may coordinate recruitment, communicate with the overseas employer, organise interviews, collect required documents and provide instructions for the work-permit and travel process.

For some jobs, applicants may need additional screening, medical examinations, training, trade tests or qualification checks. The agency should explain which requirements apply to the position and whether any preparatory steps must be completed before departure.

For the standard employer-sponsored private-sector route, the UAE employer or its authorised representative normally initiates the work-permit process through the relevant authority. Domestic-worker arrangements have their own rules and procedures.

The agency's involvement does not mean that every document it sends is proof of final approval. A recruitment receipt, job offer, work-permit application and approved entry permission are different things.

Before travelling, establish which stage your application has reached. Ask for the relevant documents and instructions on how to verify them through the responsible UAE authority.

If an agency says it has completed everything, ask what that means in practical terms. Has the employment offer been issued? Has the necessary authorisation been approved? What document permits you to enter the UAE for the intended purpose? What procedures will remain after arrival?

The answers should be specific to your job rather than a vague assurance that the agency handles all paperwork.

The job offer and contract must match

An applicant recruited through an agency should not assume that the advertised vacancy, the verbal offer and the final employment contract are interchangeable.

The written terms should establish the job title, employer, salary, contract period and other important conditions. Depending on the job, they should also clarify accommodation, transport, meals, working hours, rest days and any proposed deductions.

For applicable private-sector employment offers, the UAE provides a MoHRE Offer and Contract Inquiry service. Applicants should follow the portal's instructions to check relevant records. Where the job falls under another authority, the agency should identify that authority and explain how the offer can be verified.

Consider a worker who accepts a housekeeping job with accommodation included, only to discover that the salary in the final paperwork is lower than promised or that the position involves substantially different duties. The problem is not simply a disappointing advert. It is a discrepancy between the employment that was offered and the employment being arranged.

Ask for any changes to be explained in writing before departure. Keep copies of the original advert, written offer, signed documents and messages exchanged with the agency.

For domestic workers, the UAE's legal framework includes specific requirements concerning employment terms and recruitment arrangements. For other private-sector workers, the applicable employment rules and contract procedures must be followed.

The UAE government's guidance on job offers, work permits and employment contracts explains the general process.

How much does a UAE work visa cost through an agency?

This is one of the most important questions for applicants because the financial arrangements can differ from one recruitment route to another.

There is no single visa price that every Ugandan worker should expect to pay. The relevant rules depend on the employment category and the nature of the charge. However, applicants should not assume that every amount requested by a labour-export company is legitimate.

For ordinary employer-sponsored private-sector employment in the UAE, recruitment and employment costs are generally the employer's responsibility under the applicable rules. The UAE government's labour-rights guidance states that recruitment and travel costs, together with expenses associated with obtaining the residence permit, are to be borne by the employer with whom the worker has agreed to conclude a contract.

Domestic-worker recruitment has additional protections. UAE rules prohibit domestic-worker recruitment offices from charging workers commissions in exchange for employment. Applicants should check the rules that apply to their specific arrangement rather than treating all jobs as though they fall under the same system.

Uganda also regulates fees charged in external employment recruitment. The applicable provisions of the Employment (Recruitment of Ugandan Migrant Workers Abroad) Regulations, 2021 distinguish between recruitment arrangements in which a foreign recruitment agency pays the Ugandan agency for its services and arrangements in which charges to workers may be permitted. Where the foreign agency pays for recruitment and placement services, the regulations prohibit the Ugandan agency from charging the worker a fee under that arrangement. Separate provisions address domestic-worker recruitment and the fees that may be charged in other circumstances.

This means the question is not simply whether an agency charges money. It is what the payment covers, whether the particular charge is permitted and whether the recruitment arrangement makes the worker liable for it.

Before paying, request an itemised written explanation. Ask for the legal basis of each charge, an official receipt and confirmation of whether the overseas employer or foreign recruitment partner is already covering the recruitment costs.

Be especially cautious about large payments described as a visa quota fee, sponsorship fee, job reservation fee or guaranteed-visa charge. Do not borrow money on the strength of a verbal promise.

Some personal expenses may still arise, depending on the circumstances. Passport preparation, document services, communication and personal spending money are different from recruitment and immigration costs that the employer is legally required to cover.

If you are unsure whether a charge is lawful, check with Uganda's Ministry of Gender, Labour and Social Development before paying.

What happens before departure?

Once the job and recruitment arrangements have been verified, the agency should explain the remaining steps and what you need to do.

Depending on the vacancy, these may include submitting a passport and photographs, providing qualifications, completing screening or medical requirements, signing the appropriate employment offer and receiving travel instructions.

Do not assume that every worker needs the same documents. A domestic worker, security guard and professional employee may have different requirements. Ask the agency for the list that applies to your position, and confirm any unusual requirement independently.

Before travelling, make sure you have copies of the employment documents and the correct entry permission. Your name and passport details should match, and the document must be valid for the intended purpose.

You should also know the overseas employer's identity or the authorised arrangement under which you will work, how to contact the agency and employer, where you will report on arrival, and who will assist if the travel arrangements change.

A tourist or visit visa does not authorise you to begin working. If the agency says you can start work first and arrange the required employment permission later, do not treat that as a normal shortcut.

The agency should give you a clear explanation of the process, not merely a departure date.

After arrival: the paperwork is not necessarily finished

For the standard employer-sponsored private-sector route, the employer normally coordinates the remaining procedures after the worker enters the UAE. These may include a medical fitness examination, Emirates ID registration, employment-contract formalities and issuance of the relevant residence permit.

Domestic workers follow the applicable domestic-worker framework and associated immigration procedures.

The precise sequence varies, so the agency should explain which steps are outstanding and who is responsible for completing them. Keep copies of your passport, entry permission, employment documents and subsequent residence records.

The most important practical check is whether the job you arrive to perform matches the one you accepted.

If the salary, duties, accommodation or other material conditions differ, retain your documents and raise the issue promptly. For private-sector employment matters, the UAE's official labour-rights guidance explains how workers can seek assistance when an employer fails to provide the agreed job or pay the agreed wages.

For domestic workers, the relevant legal and support channels may differ. Establish which authority is responsible for your particular employment arrangement, and retain the Ugandan agency's contact details in case you need help.

Do not assume that you have no options simply because you travelled through a recruitment agency. Equally, do not assume that leaving an employer or changing jobs is consequence-free; understand the applicable legal procedures and obligations before taking action.

Recognising a recruitment scam

Fraudulent offers often exploit the fact that applicants are unfamiliar with overseas recruitment procedures. A recruiter may use a genuine company's name, circulate a convincing document or claim to have special access to visa approvals.

The warning signs are often found in the details.

Be cautious if someone refuses to identify the employer, cannot provide the written offer, pressures you to pay immediately or insists that independent verification will jeopardise your application. A sudden change in the promised salary or job description should also be investigated.

An agency's registration is not permission to ignore the details of a particular vacancy. Verify the company, the job and the relevant documents separately.

Keep records of payments, messages, telephone numbers, advertisements and any agreements you sign. If you suspect fraud, contact the Ugandan authorities responsible for external employment and seek guidance from the appropriate UAE labour or immigration authority.

For domestic-worker jobs, pay particular attention to recruitment charges, the contract and the arrangements for accommodation and communication. For security and other private-sector jobs, verify the actual employer, workplace and applicable employment authorisation.

The objective is not to discourage overseas work. It is to ensure that the process leading to departure is supported by documents and verifiable commitments rather than promises that cannot be enforced.

The opportunity begins with the right recruitment process

For Ugandans pursuing jobs in the UAE, a labour-export agency can be a valuable bridge between a local applicant and an overseas employer. It can make vacancies accessible, coordinate documentation and help applicants navigate a process that would otherwise be difficult to manage alone.

But the agency's involvement should make the process clearer, not exempt it from scrutiny.

Before committing to a job, establish that the recruitment company is authorised, the vacancy is genuine, the salary and working conditions are documented, and the costs being requested are lawful. Confirm which authority handles the work permit and ensure that the appropriate entry permission has been approved before travelling.

A job abroad can offer a meaningful opportunity to earn, save and support a family. The best protection against losing that opportunity to fraud or an unsuitable contract is to understand the arrangement before leaving home.
