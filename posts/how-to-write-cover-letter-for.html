How to Write a Cover Letter for an International Job

Learn how to write a cover letter for an international job, with practical examples, a ready-to-use template and work visa guidance

How to Write a Cover Letter for an International Job


Applying for a job abroad can involve months of searching, repeated applications and careful preparation of qualifications and supporting documents. After all that effort, the cover letter may be the first thing an employer reads. Yet many applicants weaken their chances by treating it as a personal introduction rather than an argument for why they should be considered.

A recruiter in London, Dubai, Toronto or Berlin is unlikely to be persuaded by a long account of an applicant's ambitions or a declaration that they are hardworking and passionate. Those qualities are claimed in thousands of applications. What matters is whether the person can perform the job, what evidence supports that claim and whether they meet the practical requirements for employment.

A good international job cover letter makes those answers clear without trying to tell the applicant's entire story. It connects relevant experience to the vacancy, explains any important circumstances surrounding the application and gives the employer a reason to read the CV.

The difference between applying abroad and applying locally

The basic purpose of a cover letter remains the same wherever the job is located. It should explain why an applicant is suitable for a particular position. International applications, however, can involve additional considerations.

An employer may need to know whether the applicant is currently based in another country, whether relocation is necessary, whether the person already has permission to work locally and whether the position is open to candidates who require sponsorship.

These details matter, but they should not overshadow professional competence.

Consider someone applying for a warehouse position in the Netherlands. Simply writing, "I am willing to relocate and work hard" says little about their suitability. A stronger application would explain their experience receiving deliveries, checking stock, operating relevant equipment or maintaining accurate inventory records. If the employer requires existing work authorisation, the applicant must also establish whether they meet that condition before investing time in the application.

The same principle applies to an administrative assistant applying to a company in Canada or a hotel worker seeking employment in the Gulf. The cover letter should lead with relevant skills and experience, not the distance the applicant is willing to travel.

Make the first paragraph specific

The opening should identify the job and establish the applicant's strongest relevant qualification or experience. If the advertisement includes a vacancy reference, include it. A specific reason for applying can also help distinguish the letter from a generic application sent to dozens of employers.

For example:

"I am applying for the Warehouse Assistant position, reference WH-104. I have two years of experience receiving deliveries, checking stock records and preparing orders, and I am interested in bringing that experience to your distribution team."

This opening is direct and informative. It gives the recruiter a job title, a relevant background and an immediate connection between the applicant and the work.

Compare it with the familiar alternative: "I am a highly motivated, hardworking and dedicated individual seeking an opportunity to utilise my skills in your esteemed organisation."

The second version is not necessarily false, but it could describe almost anyone. It consumes valuable space without providing evidence.

Applicants with limited formal experience can still write a convincing opening. Relevant vocational training, internships, volunteering, personal projects and responsibilities in previous jobs may demonstrate useful skills. The important thing is to describe those experiences honestly rather than inflate them into professional achievements they were not.

Evidence carries more weight than adjectives

The middle of the letter should demonstrate how the applicant's experience relates to the employer's needs. This is where a little research into the vacancy pays off.

Read the job description and identify the responsibilities that appear most important. Then select one or two examples from your own background that demonstrate your ability to handle them.

An applicant for a customer service role, for instance, might explain that they regularly handled customer enquiries, resolved routine complaints and updated records using a particular software system. Someone applying for a maintenance position could describe equipment they have serviced, safety procedures they have followed or faults they have helped diagnose.

Where a genuine result can be measured, include it. Processing 40 orders a day or maintaining records for a team of 15 gives an employer a clearer sense of the work involved than a claim of excellent organisational skills. There is no need to manufacture impressive numbers, however. A precise description of a responsibility is more credible than an invented achievement.

The letter should complement the CV rather than reproduce it. The CV provides the broader employment history; the cover letter explains why selected parts of that history matter for this particular vacancy.

This distinction becomes especially important when applying across industries or countries. A candidate moving from retail into customer support, for example, can explain how handling complaints, explaining products and managing transactions has prepared them for a role centred on customer communication. The connection needs to be demonstrated, not left for the recruiter to guess.

Work authorisation: be clear without making promises

For applicants seeking employment in another country, immigration requirements can be as important as professional qualifications. A strong cover letter cannot compensate for an ineligible application.

If you already have permission to work in the destination country, state your status accurately when it is relevant. If you need employer sponsorship, say so where appropriate and establish whether the vacancy can accommodate it.

An applicant based overseas might write:

"I am currently based in [Country] and would require the appropriate work authorisation for this position. I would welcome the opportunity to discuss the recruitment and relocation process if my application is successful."

That is sufficient for many applications. A detailed account of immigration rules rarely belongs in the cover letter itself.

Requirements vary considerably between destinations. The UK's Skilled Worker route, for example, involves an eligible job and an approved sponsor, alongside other applicable conditions. Canada's employer-specific work permits have their own requirements, and some cases involve a Labour Market Impact Assessment. Other countries operate different sponsorship, residence and employment-permit systems.

An applicant should consult the relevant government's immigration guidance before applying. A job offer does not automatically confer permission to work, and a tourist or visitor visa should not be assumed to permit employment.

There is also a practical trade-off. Applying for vacancies that explicitly require existing work authorisation may be futile if you do not qualify. On the other hand, excluding every employer that does not prominently advertise sponsorship could mean overlooking opportunities where international recruitment is possible. Read the actual eligibility conditions and ask the employer or authorised recruiter when the position is unclear.

Relocation should be a practical detail, not an emotional appeal

Employers understand that international candidates may need time to relocate, arrange accommodation or complete administrative requirements. What they need is a realistic indication of availability, not a promise that the applicant can arrive immediately under any circumstances.

If the employer asks about relocation, explain whether you are prepared to move and provide a realistic timeframe when you can substantiate one. Do not promise a start date that depends on a visa decision or other unresolved requirement.

Likewise, there is little value in explaining how urgently you need the job, how difficult life has been or how much you admire the destination country. Those circumstances may be personally important, but they do not establish suitability for employment.

A professional application respects the distinction between the applicant's reasons for wanting the job and the employer's reasons for offering it.

A cover letter template for an international job

The following example can be adapted for different industries and destinations. It should be rewritten for each vacancy rather than submitted unchanged.

Dear [Hiring Manager or Recruitment Team],

I am applying for the [Exact Job Title] position, reference [Vacancy Number], at [Company Name]. I am a [Relevant Professional Background or Qualification] currently based in [Country], with experience in [Relevant Area]. I am interested in this position because [Specific Reason Connected to the Role or Employer].

In my [Current or Previous Role], I [Describe a Relevant Responsibility or Achievement]. This experience has prepared me to contribute to [Specific Requirement in the Job Description]. I also have experience in [Second Relevant Skill], demonstrated through [Brief Example or Evidence].

I understand that the position is based in [Destination]. My current work-authorisation position is [Accurate Statement], and I am prepared to follow the applicable recruitment and relocation process.

I have attached my CV and [Other Requested Documents] for your consideration. Thank you for your time. I would welcome the opportunity to discuss my suitability for the position.

Kind regards,
[Full Name]
[Professional Email Address]
[Telephone Number]

Not every application needs the paragraph about relocation or work authorisation. Include it when it helps answer a genuine question about your eligibility or availability. If the employer specifies a different format, follow those instructions.

The details that can undermine an otherwise strong application

A cover letter does not need elaborate formatting. One page is generally sufficient, with short paragraphs, readable text and a professional appearance. Use the employer's name when it can be verified; otherwise, a neutral greeting is preferable to guessing.

Check that the job title, dates, qualifications and achievements agree with the CV. Follow the requested file format and naming conventions, and make sure the correct documents are attached. If the employer requests a CV and certificates but no cover letter, sending extra material may not improve the application.

Applicants should also be cautious about the recruitment channel itself. A polished vacancy advertisement does not prove that an overseas job is genuine. Guaranteed visas, guaranteed employment in exchange for large upfront payments and pressure to transfer money quickly deserve particular scrutiny.

Where recruitment agencies or labour export companies are involved, verify their authorisation through the relevant government body in the country where they operate. For example, applicants in Uganda can consult the External Employment Management Information System. Applicants elsewhere should use the equivalent official recruitment or labour authority available to them.

Never place passport numbers, bank details, passwords or one-time verification codes in a cover letter. Supply sensitive documents only when legitimately required through an appropriate, verifiable process.

Ultimately, an international cover letter is not a request for an employer to take a chance on someone's dreams. It is a concise explanation of what that person can contribute, supported by evidence and grounded in the realities of the job.

The strongest applications recognise both sides of the decision. They give employers a clear reason to consider the candidate while showing that the candidate understands the role, the recruitment process and the requirements of working abroad. That combination will not guarantee an interview, but it gives the application a more credible foundation than generic claims of ambition ever could.
