Can You Take Your Family With You on a Work Visa?

Can you take your spouse and children abroad on a work visa? Learn the rules, costs, documents and options for Ugandans moving overseas.

SSEGUPost2026-10-19



For someone in Uganda considering a job abroad, the offer letter is often only the beginning. There is the salary to examine, the recruitment process to verify, the cost of travel to calculate and, if a family is involved, a more personal question to settle: will a spouse and children be allowed to come along?

The answer depends on the country and the type of work visa. Some immigration routes allow workers to bring eligible family members from the outset. Others permit them to join later, impose financial conditions or restrict family applications for particular occupations. A job offer, even from a legitimate employer, does not automatically give a worker's family the right to live in the destination country.

That distinction can change the value of an overseas job considerably. A salary that looks attractive when considered for one person may be less appealing when it must cover rent, childcare, school expenses and the cost of supporting relatives who remain in Uganda. Equally, a job that initially requires a period of separation may offer a realistic route to relocating the whole household later.

The important thing is to establish what the visa actually permits before making arrangements around what a recruiter, employer or friend says is possible.

The job may qualify when the family does not

Immigration authorities generally treat the worker's permission and the family's permission as separate matters. The principal applicant qualifies under a particular employment route, while a spouse or child must meet the conditions for the relevant dependant visa or other immigration status.

A legally married spouse and dependent children are commonly eligible family members, but the precise definitions differ between countries. Some routes recognise qualifying unmarried partners, while others require specific evidence of a durable relationship. Parents and siblings generally cannot simply be added to an ordinary work visa as dependants.

Occupation can be just as important as the relationship. A country may allow an engineer, nurse or other eligible professional to bring a family while imposing restrictions on workers in certain temporary or lower-skilled roles. Salary thresholds, the duration of the work permit and the date an application was made may also matter.

This is why broad claims such as “the country allows workers to bring their families” can be misleading. The relevant question is whether the worker's exact visa category permits it.

What the rules look like in practice

The United Kingdom illustrates how much the answer can depend on the job itself. Eligible Skilled Worker visa holders can generally bring a partner and dependent children, but restrictions affect some occupations. Care workers and senior care workers face restrictions on bringing dependants from overseas, subject to limited exceptions. Certain medium-skilled occupations are also affected, with transitional arrangements and other exceptions applying in some circumstances.

Under the standard UK Skilled Worker dependant rules, the stated maintenance amounts are £285 for a partner, £315 for the first child and £200 for each additional child. The main applicant normally has a separate maintenance requirement of £1,270. Exemptions and sponsor-certified maintenance arrangements may apply. These amounts demonstrate access to specified funds; they are not estimates of what a family will actually spend after arrival. Visa fees and the Immigration Health Surcharge can add substantially to the initial cost.

Eligible Skilled Worker dependants can generally work in the UK, subject to restrictions on certain activities. For a family weighing whether one parent should travel first, that possibility may be an important part of the longer-term financial calculation.

Canada requires a different assessment. A foreign worker's spouse does not automatically qualify for an open work permit. Since 21 January 2025, eligibility for family open work permits has been restricted. Under the general rules, factors include the principal worker's occupation, the applicable occupational category and the remaining validity of the work authorisation. Separate programmes may have different conditions.

Children also need their own appropriate immigration status. They do not generally qualify for an open work permit under the same family-member measures, and their ability to enter or study in Canada depends on the applicable requirements.

Australia offers employer-sponsored pathways under which eligible partners and dependent children can accompany the worker or apply later. The Skills in Demand visa, subclass 482, has a subsequent-entrant process for eligible family members. But the requirements are tied to the relevant visa arrangements, and applicants should not assume that every Australian work visa provides identical family rights.

New Zealand similarly allows many work visa holders to support applications for partners and dependent children, although the available family visas and their conditions depend on the principal worker's immigration category.

The official guidance for each route is more useful than a general promise that dependants are permitted

The real cost begins before the flight

Consider a Ugandan worker offered employment in a foreign city. The employer will pay for the worker's ticket, and the monthly salary appears sufficient to cover ordinary expenses. The worker hopes to take a spouse and two children along.

The employer's offer may still leave most of the relocation bill with the family.

Each applicant may need a separate visa application, and the family may have to pay for passports, civil documents, translations, medical examinations or biometrics where required. Air tickets are only one part of the calculation. There may also be a housing deposit, temporary accommodation, furniture, transport and several weeks of living expenses before the household settles into a routine.

Children introduce costs that can be easy to underestimate from a distance. School access and fees depend on the destination and, in some countries, the region or immigration status. Childcare can be expensive, particularly if both parents intend to work. Healthcare arrangements also vary, even in countries with established public healthcare systems.

For the UK, eligible Skilled Worker dependants generally pay the applicable visa fee and Immigration Health Surcharge unless an exemption applies. The maintenance amounts specified by the government are separate from these charges and from the household's actual cost of living.

For a Ugandan family, it is sensible to calculate the budget in pounds, Canadian dollars, Australian dollars or the relevant local currency before converting it into UGX. Use a current exchange rate, account for transfer fees and allow for fluctuations. An estimate based on an old exchange rate can make an already tight budget look more comfortable than it is.

The employer should be asked, in writing, exactly what the package covers. Paying for the principal worker's flight does not necessarily mean paying for a spouse's visa, children's tickets or the family's accommodation. If relocation assistance is part of the offer, the contract or a separate written agreement should spell out its scope.

Travelling together is not always the easiest option

When the visa permits it and the finances are in place, moving together can spare a family a prolonged period of separation. Parents can settle their children into school, establish a household and adapt to the new country as a unit.

But moving everyone at once also concentrates the risk. If accommodation is unsuitable, the job turns out to be different from what was promised or the first salary is delayed, several people are affected at the same time. A worker who has not yet experienced the local cost of living may find it difficult to judge how much money the family will need.

Some families therefore consider having the worker travel first and bring the others later. This can spread the initial expenses and give the worker time to establish employment and find appropriate accommodation. It may also make it easier to understand school locations, transport costs and the practicalities of daily life before the children arrive.

That approach is not automatically available, however. The visa route must permit later dependant applications, and the family must still meet the relevant requirements. Some employers may also have sponsorship procedures to complete before dependants can join.

Nor should a family assume that the spouse's earnings will immediately solve the budget. Even where a dependant is permitted to work, finding a suitable job may take time. Qualifications may need recognition, childcare may limit working hours and the local labour market may offer fewer opportunities than expected.

A prudent household budget should therefore work on the principal worker's income alone, at least initially. A second income can improve the position later, but it is risky to treat it as guaranteed before either the immigration permission or the employment is secured.

The documents can decide how quickly the family moves

Family applications often depend on proving relationships as well as satisfying immigration conditions. A marriage certificate may establish a legally recognised marriage; children's birth certificates can establish parentage; and an unmarried partner may need evidence that meets the destination's definition of a qualifying relationship.

Where a child is travelling with one parent, custody documents or evidence of consent may be required, depending on the circumstances and the destination's rules. Names and dates should match across passports and supporting records. If they do not, the applicant may need additional evidence explaining the discrepancy.

Translations and certification are another potential source of delay. A document issued in Uganda is not necessarily accepted in its original form by every immigration authority. Applicants should check whether certified translations or particular forms of certification are required before paying for services they may not need.

Each family member must also have the appropriate immigration permission. A child's application is not necessarily covered by the parent's approval, and a family member's visa may have its own expiry date and conditions.

These details are easier to address before departure than after the worker has started employment overseas and the family is waiting in Uganda.

Your work visa is not a guarantee that your spouse can also work

The distinction between joining a worker and being allowed to earn an income is especially important for families planning to rely on two salaries.

In some countries, eligible dependants receive permission to work with relatively few restrictions. Elsewhere, a spouse may need to apply separately for a work permit, and eligibility can depend on the principal worker's occupation, permit duration or immigration category.

Even when employment is permitted, the conditions should be read carefully. A dependant may have an open work permit or permission limited to particular activities. The rules can also affect study, self-employment or work in certain occupations.

The practical question is whether the family can manage while the spouse looks for work. A professional who must obtain local registration, for example, may not be able to begin practising immediately. A parent caring for young children may also have less time available for employment than the household initially expected.

Schooling and healthcare deserve the same attention. Before relocating children, parents should establish the rules for school admission, any applicable fees, available childcare and access to medical care. A visa that permits the family to reside in a country does not necessarily settle all those arrangements.

What Ugandans should verify before accepting an offer

Overseas recruitment can involve several parties: the Ugandan recruitment agency, the foreign employer, a sponsor and an immigration authority. Their roles are not interchangeable. An agency may help arrange employment, but it cannot guarantee that the immigration authority will approve a dependant's application.

Before paying recruitment fees or making travel commitments, verify the employer and the job, obtain the written contract, identify the precise visa category and check the dependant rules through the destination country's official immigration website.

Ugandans using labour-export agencies can consult the Ministry of Gender, Labour and Social Development's External Employment Management Information System for information on recruitment companies and available jobs. Checking an agency's registration is a useful safeguard, but it does not replace independent checks on the employer, contract and visa arrangements.

Ask the employer whether family applications can be made alongside the worker's application or only after arrival. Establish who pays the application fees and travel expenses, whether accommodation is suitable for a family, and what happens if the worker changes jobs or loses employment.


Most importantly, do not treat a recruiter saying “you can bring your family” as proof. Ask which visa category permits it and check the answer against official guidance. Avoid buying non-refundable tickets before the necessary permissions have been granted.

The decision is about the household, not just the job

An overseas job can improve a family's prospects, but a higher salary does not automatically translate into a better financial position. Rent, food, transport, childcare and healthcare can absorb a substantial share of income, while obligations in Uganda may continue. Some families will find that moving together makes sense; others may prefer to establish employment and accommodation before bringing children over.

Neither arrangement is universally right. What matters is understanding the immigration conditions and the financial consequences before making a decision that affects the entire household.

For Ugandans considering work abroad, the first step is not to assume that family relocation is either guaranteed or impossible. It is to identify the exact work visa, confirm which family members qualify, understand the permission each person will receive and calculate what the move will cost.

A job offer may open the door to another country. Whether the whole family can walk through it depends on the rules, the resources available and the plans made before departure.
